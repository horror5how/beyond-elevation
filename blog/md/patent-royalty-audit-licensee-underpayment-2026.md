# 63% of Patent Licensees Underpay. The Royalty Audit Playbook That Recovers What You Are Owed.

63% of patent licensees underpay royalties. Not occasionally. Consistently.

Across technology licensing programs, independent audits reveal that nearly two-thirds of licensees underreport sales, miscalculate the royalty base, or apply unauthorized deductions — and the average underpayment runs 14 to 22% of total owed royalties. Most licensors never discover this because they never run a patent royalty audit. Hayat Amin puts it bluntly: the license agreement is where you create the revenue, and the royalty audit is where you collect it. Skip the second step and you leave 15 to 20 cents on every dollar on the table.

A patent royalty audit is the most underfunded function in IP monetization. Companies spend six figures negotiating the license and zero reviewing whether the licensee is paying correctly. This is not an oversight — it is a structural failure that compounds every quarter the books go unexamined.

## What Is a Patent Royalty Audit?

A patent royalty audit is a formal review of a licensee's financial records to verify that royalty payments match the obligations defined in the license agreement. It examines reported sales volumes, royalty base calculations, permitted deductions, and payment timing against the actual contract terms. [Beyond Elevation](https://beyondelevation.com) treats the royalty audit as the critical second half of any licensing program — the enforcement mechanism that keeps the revenue model honest.

Most licensing agreements include an audit clause that grants the licensor the right to inspect the licensee's books, typically once per year, with 30 to 60 days' notice. The audit is conducted by an independent third-party accountant, and the licensee bears the cost if the audit reveals underpayment above a threshold — usually 5 to 10%.

The problem is that fewer than 12% of licensors exercise their audit rights in any given year. This creates a moral hazard: licensees know the probability of being audited is low, so the incentive to self-correct errors or resolve ambiguities in the licensor's favour is zero.

## Why Do 63% of Licensees Underpay Patent Royalties?

Licensees underpay patent royalties for four systematic reasons, and most underpayment is not fraud. It is the predictable result of ambiguity in the license agreement that the licensee's accounting team interprets in its own favour every single quarter.

**Cause 1: Royalty base ambiguity.** The license defines royalties as a percentage of net revenue or net sales — but the definition of what counts as the royalty-bearing product, which SKUs are in scope, and how bundled products are allocated is rarely precise enough to eliminate judgment calls. Licensees make those calls in their favour. This is the definitional leak — the gap between what the negotiator intended and what the accountant calculates.

**Cause 2: Unauthorized deductions.** License agreements typically allow deductions for returns, shipping, and taxes. In practice, licensees deduct marketing costs, volume discounts, warranty reserves, and intercompany transfer pricing adjustments that the agreement never authorized. These deductions compound silently — each one shaves 1 to 3% off the royalty base.

**Cause 3: Product bundling.** When a licensee bundles a royalty-bearing product with non-royalty products, the allocation of revenue between the two is almost always skewed toward the non-royalty component. A $100 bundle where the patented component should be $60 gets reported as $35 — and the royalty is calculated on $35 instead of $60.

**Cause 4: Late and incomplete reporting.** Licensees that report quarterly often submit incomplete data — missing product lines, excluded geographies, or delayed recognition of sales made through channel partners. By the time the data arrives, the licensor has moved on. Each missed line item is lost revenue that never gets questioned.

## How Do You Run a Patent Royalty Audit? The 5-Step Royalty Audit Recovery Framework

A structured patent royalty audit follows five steps that convert a passive licensing program into an active revenue recovery operation. Hayat Amin's Royalty Audit Recovery Framework turns what most licensors treat as an adversarial legal exercise into a systematic financial process that recovers underpaid royalties while preserving the commercial relationship.

**Step 1: Trigger assessment.** Not every license warrants an audit every year. Prioritize licensees where reported royalties have flatlined despite their public revenue growth, where reports arrive late or with missing product categories, or where the license is older than three years and has never been audited. The first audit after a long gap almost always produces the largest recovery.

**Step 2: Document collection.** Issue the audit notice per the agreement's requirements — typically 30 to 60 days' written notice specifying the audit period and scope. Request the licensee's general ledger entries, sales reports by product line and geography, deduction schedules, and internal royalty calculation worksheets. The calculation worksheets are the most revealing document — they show exactly how the licensee's finance team interprets the license terms.

**Step 3: Forensic reconciliation.** The independent auditor maps every royalty payment back to the underlying sales data, line by line. This step reveals misclassified products, unreported SKUs, unauthorized deductions, and timing discrepancies. Beyond Elevation's reconciliation process typically identifies 60 to 70% of the total underpayment at this stage alone.

**Step 4: Variance analysis.** Quantify the gap between what was paid and what was owed. Break variances into three categories: definitional disputes where the licensee interpreted a term differently, computational errors involving wrong rates or base amounts, and outright omissions of unreported products or territories. This categorization determines the recovery strategy — errors are recovered immediately while definitional disputes may require an agreement amendment.

**Step 5: Recovery negotiation.** Present the audit findings to the licensee with supporting documentation. Hayat Amin's approach is direct: lead with the data, not the accusation. Most licensees prefer to settle the underpayment and amend the agreement going forward rather than litigate — especially when the audit evidence is clean and the auditor is independent. The negotiation typically resolves within 60 to 90 days, and recovery rates on confirmed underpayments exceed 85%.

## What Are the Red Flags That Signal Licensee Underpayment?

Three red flags signal that a patent royalty audit should be triggered immediately. Any one of these patterns indicates a high probability of underpayment, and all three together make an audit commercially necessary.

**Flat royalty payments despite licensee growth.** When a licensee's public revenue grows 20% year-over-year but royalty payments stay flat or decline, the math does not work. Either the licensee is shifting revenue to non-royalty categories, or the royalty base is being calculated on a shrinking subset of the actual product revenue.

**Reporting delays and format changes.** Licensees that switch reporting formats, delay submissions, or suddenly provide less granular product-line breakdowns are often reclassifying products to reduce the royalty base. The operational friction of changing formats creates cover for material changes in how royalties are calculated.

**No audit in three or more years.** The longer a license goes unaudited, the larger the cumulative underpayment. Errors compound quarterly. A licensee that has paid correctly for year one but drifts by 2% per quarter is underpaying by 8% in year two and 16% in year three. The audit clause exists for exactly this reason — use it.

## How Much Can a Patent Royalty Audit Recover?

A well-executed patent royalty audit typically recovers 14 to 22% of cumulative underpaid royalties across the audited period. The median recovery for technology licensing programs sits at 18% of total royalties owed, and some audits uncover underpayments exceeding 40% — particularly where bundled products or multi-territory licenses are involved.

For a licensing program generating $500,000 per year in royalties, an 18% recovery on a three-year audit period means $270,000 in back-payments — plus corrected calculations going forward. Hayat Amin reminds founders that the forward correction is often worth more than the back-payment: a 15% underpayment fixed at year three saves 15% every year for the remaining life of the [patent licensing revenue model](/blog/posts/patent-licensing-revenue-model/).

The cost of the audit itself — typically $15,000 to $50,000 for an independent accounting firm — is usually recoverable from the licensee if the underpayment exceeds the contractual threshold. The ROI on a royalty audit routinely exceeds 5:1, making it the single highest-return activity in any [recurring patent revenue](/blog/posts/recurring-patent-revenue-streams/) program.

Companies that set [royalty rates](/blog/posts/patent-royalty-rates-founders-underprice/) correctly but never audit are building a revenue model with a permanent leak. The rate negotiation creates the ceiling. The audit ensures you actually collect it.

## FAQ

### How often should you audit patent licensees?

Audit your highest-value licensees annually and all other licensees at least once every three years. The license agreement typically specifies the maximum audit frequency — most allow one audit per year with 30 to 60 days' notice. Prioritize licensees whose reported royalties diverge from their public revenue trajectory.

### Does running a royalty audit damage the licensing relationship?

A professional, third-party audit does not damage the relationship — it strengthens it. Licensees expect audit rights to be exercised. The damage comes from years of silence followed by a confrontational demand. Regular audits normalize the process and reduce the adversarial dynamic. Hayat Amin argues that the licensor who never audits signals that the royalty payment is optional.

### What audit rights should be in every patent license agreement?

Every license agreement should include the right to audit at least once per year, a requirement that the licensee maintain records for a minimum of three years, the right to use an independent third-party auditor, a cost-shifting clause where the licensee pays for the audit if underpayment exceeds 5 to 10%, and access to underlying sales data rather than just summary reports.

### Can you audit a licensee without an audit clause?

Without a contractual audit clause, your options are limited. You can request voluntary disclosure, but the licensee has no obligation to comply. This is why the audit clause must be negotiated into the original license agreement — it is not something you can add after the fact without the licensee's consent. If your existing licenses lack audit rights, negotiate them into the next renewal or amendment.

### What does a patent royalty audit cost?

A patent royalty audit by an independent accounting firm typically costs $15,000 to $50,000 depending on the complexity of the license, the number of product lines, and the audit period length. Most license agreements include a cost-shifting provision that makes the licensee pay if the audit reveals underpayment above a defined threshold. The ROI on a well-targeted audit routinely exceeds 5:1.
