No Claim Chart, No Revenue: The 5-Step Evidence-of-Use Process That Turns Dead Patents Into Licensing Deals

Hayat Amin CEO of Beyond Elevation · IP strategy & licensing
Hayat Amin 2026-08-21

94% of granted patents never generate a dollar of licensing revenue. The owner files, pays maintenance fees for twenty years, and watches the certificate collect dust. The gap between a granted patent and a signed licensing deal is one document most patent owners never build: the evidence-of-use claim chart.

Hayat Amin argues that a patent without a claim chart is a cost center pretending to be an asset. Across hundreds of IP advisory engagements at Beyond Elevation, the pattern is the same: founders hold granted patents they believe are valuable, but they cannot point to a single company infringing a single claim with a single piece of evidence. That is not a licensing opportunity. That is a guess. The patent claim chart turns the guess into proof — and the proof into revenue.

What Is a Patent Claim Chart?

A patent claim chart is a side-by-side document that maps every element of a patent claim to specific, publicly available evidence showing how a target product or service practices that element. It is the foundational document of every patent licensing campaign, every enforcement action, and every acquisition due diligence process where IP value is at stake.

Think of the claim chart as a patent's proof-of-relevance document. The patent itself describes what you invented. The claim chart proves that someone else is using what you invented — element by element, with evidence a licensing target cannot dispute. Without one, you are asking a potential licensee to take your word for it. Licensees do not pay based on your word. They pay based on evidence.

The format is straightforward: a table with two columns. The left column lists each element of a patent claim, broken into its constituent limitations. The right column provides evidence — screenshots, product documentation, technical specifications, marketing materials, API documentation, source code references — showing how the target's product or service meets each limitation. When every row is filled with specific, verifiable evidence, you have a licensable claim chart.

Why Do Most Patents Never Generate Licensing Revenue?

Most patents never generate licensing revenue because the owner cannot demonstrate who infringes, how they infringe, or why the infringement matters commercially. The patent claim chart is the mechanism that bridges this gap — and most patent owners skip it entirely.

The typical startup patent journey ends at grant. Founders celebrate the allowance notice, file the certificate, and move on. They assume the patent's existence alone creates deterrence or value. It does not. A granted patent without evidence of use is a legal right with no practical leverage. No licensing target will voluntarily pay royalties because you wave a patent number at them. They will pay when you demonstrate — with specificity they cannot refute — that their product practices your claims.

The numbers confirm the gap. According to IP analytics data, fewer than 6% of US patents have ever been licensed or asserted. The remaining 94% generate zero revenue across their entire twenty-year life. The difference is not patent quality. Many unlicensed patents cover genuinely valuable technology. The difference is that nobody built the evidence package to prove it. That evidence package starts with the claim chart.

What Are the 5 Steps to Build a Licensable Patent Claim Chart?

Building a licensable claim chart requires five steps: selecting your strongest claims, identifying target products, mapping each claim element to public evidence, grading the strength of each mapping, and packaging the chart for a licensing approach. Beyond Elevation calls this Hayat Amin's Claim Chart Monetization Method — the framework that converts granted patents into revenue-generating instruments.

Step 1: Select the Strongest Claims

Not every claim in your patent is worth charting. Start with independent claims — particularly the broadest independent claim that covers commercially relevant technology. A strong licensing claim has three characteristics: it maps to observable product behavior (not hidden server-side processes), it covers technology the target cannot easily design around, and it applies to a product category generating meaningful revenue for the target.

Most patents contain 15 to 25 claims. A focused claim chart starts with one to three independent claims. Charting every claim wastes time and dilutes impact. Focus creates leverage.

Step 2: Identify Target Products

The claim chart targets a specific product, not a company in general. List every product, service, or feature from the potential licensee that might practice your claims. Be specific: name the product version, the feature set, and the market segment. A claim chart against "Google's search advertising platform" is weaker than one against "Google Ads Smart Bidding as implemented in Performance Max campaigns, version Q2 2026."

Step 3: Map Each Claim Element to Public Evidence

This is where most patent owners fail. Every limitation in the claim must be mapped to specific, publicly available evidence from the target. Acceptable evidence includes product documentation, user manuals, API references, published white papers, marketing materials, technical blog posts, patent applications filed by the target describing the same technology, product screenshots, and recorded demonstrations.

The standard is specificity. "The target's product likely uses machine learning" is not evidence. "The target's product documentation at [URL] states: 'Our recommendation engine uses a transformer-based model trained on user interaction sequences'" is evidence. Every row in the claim chart must meet this standard.

Step 4: Grade the Strength of Each Mapping

After mapping every element, grade each row. Industry practitioners use a three-tier system: strong (direct public evidence confirms the element), moderate (public evidence strongly implies the element but does not explicitly confirm it), and weak (the element is likely practiced but evidence is circumstantial). A licensable claim chart needs every element mapped at strong or moderate. A single weak element in an independent claim undermines the entire chart.

Step 5: Package for the Licensing Approach

A raw claim chart is a legal document. A licensing package is a business document. Package the claim chart with a cover memo that summarizes the infringement position in plain language, quantifies the commercial relevance of the patented technology to the target's business, provides a royalty basis grounded in comparable licenses or established royalty rate methodologies, and offers a licensing path that is cheaper and faster than the alternative. The package should make the business case for licensing in three pages before the recipient ever opens the claim chart itself.

How Does a Claim Chart Change a Licensing Negotiation?

A claim chart shifts a licensing negotiation from speculation to evidence. Instead of approaching a potential licensee with a vague infringement assertion, you present a document showing exactly how their product practices each element of your claim — supported by their own public documentation.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that licensing negotiations follow a predictable pattern: the first meeting determines whether the target takes you seriously or files your letter in the circular bin. A well-built claim chart makes the first meeting productive because the target's engineers cannot dismiss the analysis. They can debate scope. They can negotiate rate. But they cannot pretend the overlap does not exist when their own documentation proves it does.

The data supports this. Patent licensing campaigns backed by strong claim charts close at rates between 3% and 7% of relevant revenue, according to licensing revenue benchmarks. Campaigns without claim charts — relying on cease-and-desist letters and vague infringement allegations — close below 0.5% of the time. The claim chart is not optional. It is the licensing campaign.

What Separates a Revenue-Ready Claim Chart from a Worthless One?

The difference between a claim chart that generates licensing revenue and one that sits in a drawer is specificity. Revenue-ready claim charts map every claim element to publicly verifiable evidence from the target's own products, documentation, or marketing materials. Worthless claim charts use generic industry descriptions, assume how the target's technology works, or leave elements unmapped.

Three factors separate the two categories. First, evidence sourcing: revenue-ready charts cite specific URLs, document versions, and publication dates. A licensing target's counsel will verify every source. Broken links, outdated references, or paywalled sources weaken the chart. Second, claim selection: charting a dependent claim that adds a minor limitation while ignoring the broader independent claim wastes the analysis. Hayat Amin's approach prioritises the broadest commercially relevant independent claim first, then adds dependent claims only when they strengthen the infringement position against a specific target. Third, commercial framing: the claim chart must connect to revenue. Showing that a target's product infringes is necessary. Showing that the infringing feature drives $200M in annual revenue for the target makes the licensing conversation real.

Companies with patents are 10.2x more likely to secure early-stage funding — but that statistic rewards the patent itself, not the licensing infrastructure around it. The claim chart is where the patent becomes a recurring revenue instrument rather than a one-time credibility marker.

When Should You Build Your First Claim Chart?

Build your first evidence-of-use claim chart within 90 days of patent grant. Waiting years to chart your claims is the single largest destroyer of patent licensing value — not because the patent weakens, but because the evidence landscape shifts. Products change. Documentation gets updated. Features get renamed or deprecated. The evidence that proves infringement today may not exist in 18 months.

Hayat Amin says the 90-day window after grant is the optimal strike zone: your technology is still current, target products are documented, and you can build a licensing pipeline before competitors file around your claims. Every quarter you wait past grant is a quarter where your claim chart evidence degrades and your licensing leverage diminishes.

FAQ

How much does it cost to build a patent claim chart?

A professional evidence-of-use claim chart costs between $3,000 and $15,000 per patent, depending on claim complexity and the number of targets mapped. In-house charts built by the inventor cost less upfront but often lack the evidentiary rigour licensing targets require. The return on a well-built claim chart — measured against licensing revenue generated — typically exceeds 20x the investment within 18 months.

How long does it take to build a claim chart?

A thorough claim chart takes two to four weeks per patent-target pair. The time is spent on evidence sourcing, not analysis. Finding specific, publicly available evidence for every claim element is the bottleneck. Rushing the evidence phase produces weak charts that undermine the licensing campaign.

Do I need a claim chart for every patent in my portfolio?

No. Start with the two to three patents whose claims most directly map to commercially significant products in your market. A focused licensing campaign with three strong claim charts outperforms a scattered campaign with twenty weak ones. Prioritise patents where the target's relevant product revenue exceeds $50M annually.

Can I build a claim chart without a patent attorney?

The inventor often builds the best initial claim chart because they understand the technology deeply enough to identify where it appears in target products. A patent attorney or licensing professional should then review and formalise the chart for evidentiary rigour. The combination of inventor knowledge and professional formatting produces the strongest licensing documents.

What happens after I send a claim chart to a licensing target?

A well-built claim chart typically triggers one of three responses: the target's counsel requests a meeting to discuss licensing terms, the target attempts to design around the patented features, or the target ignores the letter. Response rates for claim-chart-backed licensing approaches run between 40% and 60% — compared to under 5% for generic patent assertion letters without evidence. The claim chart forces engagement because it removes plausible deniability.
