AI consultant vs AI engineer: which should you hire first?

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-11-10

Hire an AI consultant when you don't yet know where AI pays, and hire an AI engineer when you know what to build and it has to run every day. Below is what each costs in the US in 2026, where each wins, and how to decide this week.

Hire an AI consultant when you don't yet know where AI pays in your company. Hire an AI engineer when you already know what to build and it has to run every day inside your product or your systems. Most owners need the first answer before they can use the second, and a lot of them pay for both and get neither.

Hayat Amin has spent twenty years as a technology CFO and sold three companies, so he reads this choice the way a buyer reads a cost line: what do you get for the money, and does it still work in month seven? He now does the work himself, as a forward deployed engineer inside the companies that hire him. This covers what each hire delivers, what each costs in the United States in 2026, where each one wins, and the steps to make the call this week.

AI consultant vs AI engineer: the short answer

An AI consultant sells a decision. You get a view on which processes are worth automating, which tools to buy, what the risks are and in what order to move. The good ones also set up the first tools and train your people. The output is usually a document, a roadmap or a pilot.

An AI engineer sells a working system. They write the code, connect the model to your data, test it, ship it and keep it running. The output is software in production, and it needs someone to own it after launch.

Insight Global, the staffing firm, put the split plainly in a 29 October 2026 piece: hire AI talent when AI is an ongoing capability you want to own, and use a consulting team when you have a defined problem, need several skills at once or need help choosing use cases. That matches what we see. The real mistake is hiring an engineer before anyone has decided what's worth building, or hiring a consultant and expecting code.

what each one costs in 2026

These are the numbers we could source this week, with who said them. Treat the consulting ranges as rough, because most of them are published by firms that sell consulting.

  • AI engineer, full time. Insight Global's 2026 AI Salary Guide puts the median at $150,000 a year. KORE1, a tech recruiter, puts mid-market base pay at $185,000 to $260,000 in a guide updated 21 October 2026.
  • The loaded cost of that hire. KORE1 adds 25% to 30% for benefits and payroll tax on a senior role, which turns a $200,000 engineer into $250,000 to $260,000 a year.
  • AI engineer, contract. KORE1, citing ZipRecruiter, gives $150 to $200 an hour for a senior applied AI engineer in the US. At $160 an hour for about 1,800 hours, that's $288,000 for a full year.
  • AI consultancy. Perceptive Analytics, writing on 11 August 2026, cites consulting cost analyses that put monthly retainers at $10,000 to $50,000 and scoped production projects at $150,000 to $500,000.
  • Time to start. KORE1 reports about 17 days to a signed candidate on direct IT searches, then 6 to 8 weeks before a full time hire is committing code. A contractor can start in days to two weeks.

The supply is thin too. Insight Global cites LinkedIn's September 2025 update: AI engineering hiring rose more than 25% year on year, and AI talent made up less than 1% of LinkedIn members while AI engineering jobs were nearly 7% of technical postings. If you're a 40 person company in Ohio competing for that engineer with a bank in New York, you'll feel those numbers. where the AI consultant wins

A consultant wins when the question is still "where". You've got six ideas, a board asking for an AI plan, and no one inside who can rank them. A good consultant will tell you the quoting process is worth fixing and the chatbot idea isn't, and that saves you from paying an engineer to build the wrong thing.

They also win when the work crosses functions. Compliance, finance, operations and sales all have to agree before anything is built, and an outsider can run that conversation faster than a new hire who reports to one of them.

The last mile is where they lose. A roadmap doesn't move a single invoice. If the consultant's deliverable is a slide deck and nobody inside can build it, you've paid for a plan you can't use. Ask in the first call whether the output is a recommendation or a working system, and get it in writing.

where the AI engineer wins

An engineer wins when AI is part of what you sell. If you're a SaaS company and the feature has to sit in front of customers next quarter, you need someone who owns that code for years, not months. KORE1 and Insight Global both land in the same place: own the capability in house when it's a lasting part of the product.

The operations side of a small or mid sized company is where they lose. Your problem is rarely a model. It's that HubSpot, QuickBooks and the shared inbox don't talk, and a person retypes orders between them for 10 hours a week. A $150,000 engineer hired into that company often spends the first quarter learning how the business works, with no one senior enough to tell them which problem to fix first. That's how a pilot ends up working in the demo and failing on real data, which we wrote about in why most AI pilots fail.

how to decide this week

You can make this call in about four hours, spread over five days. Do these in order.

  1. Write down the 3 to 5 tasks your team repeats every week, and the hours each one takes. Ask the people who do them, not their managers. If the total is under 10 hours a week, you don't need a hire yet.
  2. For each task, list the systems it touches, such as your CRM, your accounting software, email and a spreadsheet. A task that touches 3 or more systems is a joining problem, not a model problem.
  3. Put a dollar figure on each one. Hours a week times a loaded hourly cost times 52. Eight hours a week at $55 an hour is $22,880 a year, by our arithmetic.
  4. Answer one question. Do you know which task to fix first? If no, you need the consultant's skill. If yes, and it lives inside your product, you need the engineer's.
  5. If you know what to fix and it lives in operations, not the product, scope one workflow with a 30 day finish line and one owner inside the business. Don't hire anyone full time until that one workflow is live.
  6. Whoever you talk to, ask for one example of a system they built that's still running six months later, and who owns it now. A consultant without one is selling advice. An engineer without one hasn't shipped into a business like yours.

when it's bigger than a weekend fix

If step 2 shows that most of your hours sit in tasks that cross three or four systems nobody owns, a spreadsheet and a free trial won't close it. That's when you bring in a forward deployed engineer, who works inside your company on your real data until the workflow runs in production. Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between an AI consultant and an AI engineer?

An AI consultant decides where AI pays and in what order, and usually delivers a plan or a pilot. An AI engineer builds and runs the system in production. One sells a decision, the other sells working software.

Should I hire an AI consultant or an AI engineer first?

If you don't know which process to fix first, start with the consultant's question. If you know, and it's part of your product, hire the engineer. If it's in operations, scope one workflow with a 30 day finish line first.
