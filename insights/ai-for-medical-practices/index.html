AI for Medical Practices: 8 Operations Worth Automating First

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-10-16

AI for medical practices pays off first in the back office: the visit note, prior authorisation, eligibility, claim follow-up, intake, phones, HIPAA-safe connections and a live owner's screen. Every price below was read on the vendor's own page on 16 October 2026.

AI for medical practices pays off first in the back office, not the exam room. Eight operations are worth automating first: the visit note, prior authorisation, eligibility checks, claim follow-up, patient intake, the phones and reminders, the safe link between your systems, and a live screen for the owner. Most independent practices run all eight by hand or by fax, and the same patient gets typed in three times.

Hayat Amin has spent twenty years in technology and sold three companies as chief financial officer, so he reads a practice the way a buyer does: by its collections, its days in accounts receivable and the hours its staff lose to insurers. He now builds this work inside companies himself, as a forward deployed engineer. Every price below was read on the vendor's own page on 16 October 2026, and where a vendor hides its price, we say so.

which practices get the most from this

Three symptoms qualify a practice. A front desk person checks insurance on a payer portal and retypes the result into the practice management system. Someone spends part of every day on prior authorisations by phone and fax. And the owner can't say what was collected this week, or how many claims are stuck, until the billing company sends a report after month end.

The time loss is measured. In a time and motion study of 57 US physicians published in the Annals of Internal Medicine in 2016, physicians spent 27.0 percent of the office day face to face with patients and 49.2 percent on EHR and desk work. The authors' summary is blunt: for every hour of direct patient time, nearly 2 more hours went on EHR and desk work, plus 1 to 2 hours of personal time each night.

The staff side is growing, not shrinking. The Bureau of Labor Statistics counts 979,000 medical secretary and administrative assistant jobs in 2025, with a median wage of $45,930 in May 2025, and projects 5 percent growth to 2035. Medical records specialists number 200,700, with a median of $51,140 and 8 percent projected growth. Those are the people a practice in Houston, Chicago or Queens is competing to hire, so hours you win back are hours you don't have to recruit for.

The industry already knows the size of it. The 2025 CAQH Index, released on 19 February 2026, estimates US healthcare avoided $258 billion in administrative costs in 2024 by automating transactions, with $21 billion still on the table. It also found 25 percent of provider organizations using AI tools in administrative work, against more than 50 percent of health plans. The insurers are ahead of you, and they're the other side of every claim.

how we picked the eight

An operation made the list if a practice of 2 to 20 providers does it every week, a named product handles it today, and the time it costs is published by someone other than a vendor. Prices come from each vendor's own page, read this week. Vendor performance claims are labelled as the vendor's. athenahealth's site refused our reader every time, so athenaOne isn't assessed here. Nobody paid to appear. Beyond Elevation appears once, near the end, with the affiliation stated.

1. the visit note

This is the one physicians feel, and the easiest to buy. An ambient AI scribe listens to the visit and drafts the note. Freed lists Starter at $39 a month for up to 40 notes, and Premier at $104 a month, which adds EHR push integration and ICD-10 and CPT coding. Heidi Health has a free plan and a 14 day trial and shows no paid prices on its pricing page. Abridge runs inside Epic and sells to health systems with no published price, so it's wrong for a three doctor family practice.

The join to check is the last step. A note that has to be copied from the scribe into the EHR by hand saves the doctor's evening and costs the medical assistant's morning. Ask whether the push into your EHR is included at the plan you're buying.

2. prior authorization

The American Medical Association's 2025 survey of 1,000 practicing physicians puts the average at 40 prior authorizations a week, consuming 13 hours of physician and staff time. Forty percent of physicians employ staff who do nothing else. Ninety-five percent said prior authorization delays access to care, and 26 percent said it had led to a serious adverse event for a patient.

CAQH's 2024 Index found providers spend 24 minutes requesting an authorization by phone, fax or email and 16 minutes on a health plan portal. At 40 a week, moving from fax to portal alone is about 5.3 hours a week back, and that's our arithmetic, not CAQH's. Silna says it handles prior authorizations, benefit checks and insurance monitoring before the visit across 1,000 payers in 50 states, and publishes no price. Cohere Health is built for health plans, not practices, so it isn't one to buy.

3. eligibility and benefits before the visit

A denied claim often starts as an eligibility check nobody ran, or ran and didn't record. CAQH puts the time saved by checking eligibility electronically at 12 minutes per transaction for medical providers. Availity's Essentials portal is free to providers for the payers that sponsor it, and a paid Essentials Plus subscription covers payers that don't. Availity shows no price for Plus.

The automation is a nightly batch against tomorrow's schedule, with every failed check flagged to the front desk before the patient walks in. That only works if the result writes back to the patient's record, not to a screenshot in someone's inbox.

4. claim status and billing follow-up

Checking where a claim is takes 18 minutes less per inquiry when it's done electronically, according to CAQH, the biggest per-transaction saving of the three it measures for medical providers. CollaborateMD sells billing software whose practice plans, by its own FAQ, start at $299 per provider per month, and billing companies start at $0.32 per claim. Its interfaces with preferred partners are free.

The work worth automating is the queue: every claim older than 30 days without a status, pulled automatically, sorted by dollar value and handed to one person each morning. Right now that list lives in the biller's head.

5. patient intake and the copay

Clipboard intake means a staff member types the patient's history, insurance card and consent forms into the system after the patient sits down. Phreesia sells digital intake and payments to more than 4,700 healthcare organizations, and claims an 80 percent copay collection rate against a 56 percent industry average and an 87 percent reduction in call volume. Those are Phreesia's figures about its own customers. It says it can be live in 6 weeks and publishes no price.

Wrong for you if your patients are mostly walk-ins who won't fill a form on their phone beforehand.

6. the phones, reminders and no-shows

An MGMA Stat poll on 11 August 2026, with 190 responses, found 32 percent of medical groups seeing higher no-show rates so far in 2026 than in 2025, 58 percent about the same and 10 percent lower. Every no-show is an empty slot you already paid a provider and a room for. Weave combines phones, texting and reminders and lists pricing from $199 a month, with each plan quoted on request. Most of the examples on its page are dental, so ask for a medical reference.

The useful build sits after the reminder. A cancellation should offer the slot to the next patient on the waitlist by text, without anyone at the desk picking up the phone.

7. the safe link between your systems

This is where most practices get the automation wrong. HHS guidance says that when a cloud service creates, receives, maintains or transmits protected health information on your behalf, it's a business associate, and you need a business associate agreement with it. Zapier, the tool most owners reach for first, doesn't claim HIPAA compliance. A Zapier staff member said so plainly on its own community forum.

Keragon is built for exactly this gap. It includes a BAA on every plan, and lists Starter at $149 a month billed monthly or $99 a month billed annually, Professional at $399 or $269, and Scale Up at $1,499 or $995. Before you connect your scheduling system to anything, ask for the BAA in writing. If a vendor won't sign one, patient data can't go through it.

8. the owner's screen, live

Everything above feeds one view: visits booked and seen today, no-shows, authorizations pending, claims over 30 days, denials by payer and cash collected this week against the same week last year. In most independent practices that view is a monthly PDF from the billing company, which tells you in November what went wrong in September.

Tebra sells practice management, EHR, billing and patient experience as one product with unlimited non-clinical staff, quoted on request. It publishes only add-on prices, such as an EPCS setup charge of about $75 per provider, billed once. An all-in-one product shortens the list of joins, but the bank, the payroll and the payer portals still sit outside it. No vendor sells the screen that pulls them together, which is why we build it.

Frequently asked questions

How to use AI in medical practice?

Start where your staff lose the most hours, which for most practices is prior authorization and claim follow-up, not diagnosis. The AMA's 2025 survey puts prior authorization at 40 a week and 13 hours of physician and staff time. Automate one operation, connect it to your practice management system, and measure the hours before adding the next.

What is the best AI for medical practice?

It depends on which job costs you most. For notes, Freed starts at $39 a month for up to 40 notes. For prior authorisation, Silna covers 1,000 payers and quotes on request. For moving patient data between systems under a BAA, Keragon starts at $99 a month billed annually. Buy for the hours you can measure today.

Can AI do medical billing and coding?

Partly. Freed's Premier plan at $104 a month suggests ICD-10 and CPT codes from the visit note, and billing software can pull claim status automatically, which CAQH says saves 18 minutes per inquiry. A certified coder or biller still owns the claim and the appeal. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects medical records specialist jobs growing 8 percent to 2035.

Will AI replace medical receptionists?

No. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects medical secretary and administrative assistant jobs growing 5 percent from 2025 to 2035, while all secretaries and administrative assistants decline 2 percent. AI takes the retyping, the reminder calls and the portal checks. The person at the desk still handles the patient.

Is an AI scribe HIPAA compliant?

Only if the vendor signs a business associate agreement with you and handles patient data under it. HHS guidance treats any cloud service that stores or transmits protected health information for you as a business associate. Ask for the BAA before the first recorded visit, and check which plan it comes with.

How much does an AI scribe cost?

Freed lists $39 a month for up to 40 notes and $104 a month for Premier. Heidi Health has a free plan and shows no paid prices on its page. Abridge sells to health systems and publishes no price. Group and clinic plans at Freed and Heidi are quoted on request.
