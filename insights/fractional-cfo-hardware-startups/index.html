SaaS CFOs Break Hardware Companies. Here Is What a Hardware CFO Actually Does Differently.

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-11-05

A fractional CFO for hardware startups manages cash conversion cycles, BOM costing, and inventory carrying costs that SaaS-trained CFOs have never touched. Here is what the job looks like.

A fractional CFO for hardware startups manages cash conversion cycles, bill-of-materials costing, and inventory carrying costs that a SaaS-trained CFO has never touched. Hardware companies convert cash to inventory to revenue three to six times slower than software companies. Hire a CFO who has only optimised MRR and CAC, and your hardware startup will run out of runway before it ships.

According to a 2026 CB Insights post-mortem analysis, 38% of hardware startups that fail cite cash mismanagement as a primary cause, nearly double the rate for software companies. Hayat Amin argues the gap comes down to experience rather than intelligence: "A SaaS CFO who has never costed a bill of materials will misforecast your COGS by 15 to 30 percent. That error compounds through every unit you ship."

Why do hardware startups need a different fractional CFO?

Hardware startups need a different fractional CFO because the financial mechanics of physical products, inventory carrying costs, supplier payment terms, tooling amortisation, and warranty reserves, have no equivalent in software. A SaaS CFO optimises for monthly recurring revenue. A hardware CFO optimises for cash-to-cash cycle time.

The difference starts with the cash conversion cycle. A typical SaaS company collects payment before or at the point of delivery. A hardware company pays for raw materials 60 to 120 days before it ships a finished product, and then waits another 30 to 60 days for payment. That gap, often 90 to 180 days, is the number that kills hardware startups.

Then there is BOM complexity. A bill of materials for a single hardware SKU can run to 200 line items across 15 suppliers in four countries. Each line carries a landed cost that shifts with commodity prices, tariffs, shipping rates, and minimum order quantities. A CFO who has never managed a BOM will default to a flat COGS assumption. That assumption is wrong on day one and gets worse every quarter.

Inventory carrying costs are the third wedge. Warehousing, insurance, obsolescence risk, and working capital tied up in unsold stock typically add 20 to 30 percent on top of the unit cost. SaaS companies have zero inventory. Hardware companies can have 40 percent of their total capital locked in a warehouse. A working capital management strategy is existential, not optional.

What does a fractional CFO do for a hardware startup?

A fractional CFO for a hardware startup builds the financial model around physical product economics, BOM costing, inventory turns, supplier term negotiation, unit economics at scale, tooling amortisation, warranty reserve modelling, and cash conversion cycle optimisation. These seven functions are the job.

1. BOM costing and landed cost modelling. The CFO builds a living cost model that tracks every component, freight lane, and tariff exposure. If a capacitor price doubles because of a supply shortage, the model shows the margin impact within hours, not months.

2. Inventory turn optimisation. The target is turns per year, not units in stock. A hardware CFO sets reorder points, safety stock levels, and ABC classification to keep working capital moving. Every extra week of inventory on hand is cash that is not earning a return.

3. Supplier payment term negotiation. Extending payment terms from net-30 to net-60 on a £500K quarterly materials spend frees £250K of working capital. That is a fundraising round you did not need to do. The CFO runs this negotiation with data, not charm.

4. Unit economics at manufacturing scale. The per-unit cost at 100 units is irrelevant to the per-unit cost at 10,000 units. Tooling amortisation, volume discounts, and yield rates all change the number. The CFO models this curve so the founder knows exactly which volume breaks make the business profitable. Beyond Elevation maps these break-even curves during the first 100 days of every hardware engagement.

5. Tooling and capex amortisation. A £200K injection mould is a capital asset that amortises across every unit produced, not a one-off expense. The CFO sets the depreciation schedule, models the payback period, and shows the board the true unit cost inclusive of tooling.

6. Warranty reserve modelling. Hardware ships with implied and express warranties. The CFO models expected failure rates, average repair costs, and return rates to set aside a reserve that matches reality. Underestimate by two percentage points on a £5M revenue run and you have a £100K hole in your P&L.

7. Cash conversion cycle management. The CFO measures the gap between paying suppliers and collecting from customers, then systematically closes it. Every day removed from the cycle frees working capital. On a £2M annual materials spend, shrinking the cycle by 15 days releases roughly £82K of cash.

What are the five financial mistakes hardware startups make?

The five costliest financial mistakes hardware startups make are undercosting the BOM, ignoring minimum order quantities in the financial model, skipping warranty reserves, conflating gross margin with contribution margin, and paying suppliers too early. Each one alone can kill a fundraise or a company.

Mistake 1: Undercosting the BOM. Founders price from a prototype BOM, not a production BOM. Prototype components are sourced from distributors at retail markup. Production quantities come from contract manufacturers at negotiated rates, but add freight, tariffs, quality inspection, and yield loss. Hayat Amin's rule on this is blunt: "If your BOM cost has not changed between prototype and production, you have not done the work. It always changes. The direction depends on whether you have a CFO or a spreadsheet."

Mistake 2: Ignoring MOQs in the financial model. Minimum order quantities from component suppliers force you to buy 10,000 units of a part when you only need 2,000. The excess sits on a shelf, ties up cash, and risks obsolescence if the design changes. A fractional CFO models MOQ exposure at every production volume.

Mistake 3: No warranty reserves. Most hardware startups set warranty reserves at zero until the first returns arrive. By then the P&L is already wrong. Industry-standard warranty reserve for consumer electronics runs 2 to 5 percent of revenue. For industrial hardware, 1 to 3 percent. A CFO who has shipped physical products knows these ranges from day one.

Mistake 4: Conflating gross margin with contribution margin. A 55 percent gross margin looks strong until you realise it excludes shipping, returns, warranty costs, payment processing, and customer support. Contribution margin, the number investors care about, includes all variable costs. The gap between the two is 15 to 25 percentage points for hardware companies. A unit economics model investors actually trust shows contribution margin, not gross margin.

Mistake 5: Paying suppliers too early. Founders pay invoices the day they arrive out of fear of losing the relationship. A hardware CFO pays on the last day of the agreed term, every time. On a £1M annual spend with net-60 terms, paying on day one instead of day 60 costs the company roughly £50K in lost working capital yield. That is money you set on fire to feel polite.

When should a hardware startup hire a fractional CFO?

A hardware startup should hire a fractional CFO before its first manufacturing run, not after. The financial architecture, BOM cost model, runway model, supplier payment terms, and inventory plan, needs to be in place before capital flows to the factory floor. Retrofitting these systems after production starts costs three to five times more.

Hayat Amin's Hardware Finance Readiness Test is a five-question diagnostic that Beyond Elevation runs on every hardware founder before engagement:

1. Do you have a production BOM costed at volume, not prototype pricing? If not, your financial model is fiction.

2. Have you modelled MOQ cash exposure for every critical component? If not, your first purchase order will blow your cash plan.

3. Do you know your cash conversion cycle in days? If not, you cannot forecast when you run out of money.

4. Have you set warranty reserves based on industry failure rates? If not, your margin is overstated.

5. Do you track contribution margin, or only gross margin? If not, investors will find the gap themselves, in diligence, when it is too late to fix the narrative.

Hayat Amin showed one hardware founder that their financial model, built on prototype costs, overstated gross margins by 22 percentage points. The corrected model changed the fundraise ask from £3M to £4.5M, and the round closed because the numbers were credible, not optimistic.

If your startup is three months from a first production run, already shipping but losing money on every unit, or preparing to raise with hardware-specific financial complexity, a fractional CFO is the difference between shipping and shutting down.

FAQ

Can a SaaS fractional CFO learn hardware finance on the job?

Technically, yes. Practically, no, not on your runway. Hardware financial mistakes compound with every production run. A SaaS CFO learning BOM costing while you are shipping product is like a surgeon learning anatomy during surgery. Hire someone who has already made the expensive mistakes at another company.

Do hardware startups need a fractional CFO or someone full time?

At pre-revenue through £5M in annual revenue, fractional. The operational complexity justifies specialised finance leadership, but not 40 hours a week of it. A fractional CFO at two to three days per week covers the critical functions, BOM costing, supplier terms, cash cycle management, without the £150K to £200K fully loaded cost of hiring someone full time.

What is the biggest difference between a hardware CFO and a SaaS CFO?

Cash timing. A SaaS CFO manages a business where revenue arrives before or at delivery. A hardware CFO manages a business where cash leaves the building 90 to 180 days before revenue arrives. That gap is the entire job. Everything else, inventory, suppliers, tooling, warranty, flows from it.

How do investors evaluate a hardware startup's financial model?

Investors look for contribution margin (not gross margin), cash conversion cycle in days, BOM cost at target production volume, warranty reserves, and MOQ exposure. If your model shows only gross margin and a flat COGS assumption, the investor knows no one with hardware experience built it. That doubt kills term sheets.
