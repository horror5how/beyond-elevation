IP Strategy for 2027: The 5 Priorities Every CEO Should Set Before January

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-11-11

Most CEOs enter 2027 with no documented IP strategy priorities. These five moves, from AI ownership policy to data licensing readiness, separate funded companies from exposed ones.

Companies with patents are 10.2x more likely to secure early-stage funding, according to the European Patent Office. Yet most CEOs enter 2027 without a single documented IP priority. Hayat Amin argues that the gap between companies that plan their IP strategy and those that react to it widens every quarter, and 2027 is the year it becomes irreversible.

The five IP strategy priorities below are the ones Hayat Amin runs when onboarding a new Beyond Elevation client in Q4. They take 90 days to implement, cost a fraction of one bad patent filing, and position a company to raise, license, or exit on its own terms.

IP strategy needs a 2027 reset

IP strategy in 2027 needs a reset because the rules, technology and competition around IP moved faster in 2026 than in any year before it. Three forces converged: the EU AI Act's first enforcement provisions, a wave of court rulings on AI-generated content ownership, and a VC market that now prices defensibility above growth rate in its scoring frameworks.

Most companies are still running the IP playbook they set in 2024. That playbook did not account for AI agents generating patentable output, employees feeding trade secrets into public AI tools, or data assets becoming licensable at scale. A company entering 2027 with an unreformed IP strategy is leaving value on the table and risk on the books.

Beyond Elevation sees this pattern every quarter. Founders who treat IP as a legal checkbox instead of a strategic asset trail their competitors in every metric that matters: valuation multiple, licensing revenue, and acquirer interest. The fix is five priorities, starting now.

Priority 1: who owns what your AI tools create?

AI-generated IP ownership is the single largest unresolved question in most companies' IP portfolios, and 2027 will force an answer. The EU AI Act requires transparency disclosures for AI-generated content, and US courts have ruled that purely AI-generated works cannot receive copyright protection, but hybrid human-AI works remain in a grey zone that only a documented internal policy can resolve.

The practical risk is concrete. Your engineering team uses AI code assistants daily, your marketing team generates content with generative AI, and your product team prototypes with AI design tools. Without a written policy defining the human contribution threshold for each output type, you cannot know what you own, and what you cannot prove you own, you cannot patent, license, or defend.

Hayat Amin's rule here is direct: document the human-in-the-loop for every AI-assisted output that matters commercially. If the human contribution is insufficient for copyright or patent eligibility, the output is unprotectable, and planning around unprotectable assets is planning to lose.

Priority 2: is your data ready to license?

Data licensing readiness is the gap between having valuable data and being able to monetise it. According to Grand View Research, the data monetisation market grows from $4.8 billion in 2026 to $17.6 billion by 2033, yet most companies cannot license their data because it is not structured, registered, or legally clean enough for a counterparty to buy.

The fix is a three-step audit: inventory every proprietary dataset, confirm provenance and rights documentation for each one, and identify which datasets are licensable in their current state versus which need cleaning. A dataset with unclear provenance or mixed third-party rights is unlicensable regardless of its commercial value.

Companies that complete this audit in Q4 enter 2027 with a licensable data catalogue, an asset that sits on no balance sheet but drives real revenue. DGS completed exactly this process with Beyond Elevation, turning an internal data layer into a monetised asset that most of their board had written off as operational overhead.

Priority 3: does your patent filing roadmap match your 2027 product plan?

A patent filing roadmap that does not align with the product plan is worse than no roadmap at all, because it burns budget protecting innovations the company has moved past. Every Q4, the filing roadmap should be rebuilt from the 2027 product plan, not carried forward from the year before.

The real question is what you'll ship in 2027 that a well-funded competitor could replicate within 18 months. Those innovations get filed. Everything else goes into the trade secret programme or gets deprioritised.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that a provisional patent application costs under £3,000 and buys 12 months of priority. Filing four provisionals in January on the right innovations costs less than one full utility filing on the wrong one. The maths favours speed and precision over breadth, and a fractional Chief IP Officer knows which innovations clear the 18-month replication test and which do not.

Priority 4: will your trade secrets survive an AI tool audit?

Trade secret protection collapsed for two companies in early 2026 when US courts ruled that information entered into public AI tools lost its confidential status. In one case, a founder's own use of ChatGPT to develop a trade secret destroyed the "reasonable measures" requirement under the Defend Trade Secrets Act. The founder gave away the secret, one prompt at a time.

Hayat Amin says the lesson is blunt and most companies have not acted on it: if your employees can paste proprietary information into a public AI tool without a policy stopping them, you have a disclosure programme, not a trade secret one.

Fixing this for 2027 means writing an AI tool usage policy that spells out which tools are approved, what information staff can and cannot enter, and what happens if they break the rule. Pair it with an approved-tools list and a quarterly audit. Companies that brought this in after Beyond Elevation's trade secret reviews found an average of seven undisclosed AI tool accounts per team, each one a potential leak.

Priority 5: who owns your IP strategy?

The first four priorities fail without someone owning them. A Chief IP Officer (fractional or full-time) is the executive who turns IP from a legal cost into a line of revenue. Without one, the patent filing roadmap drifts, the data licensing audit stalls, and the trade secret policy sits in a shared drive nobody opens.

Most companies under £50 million in revenue don't need a CIPO full time. A fractional Chief IP Officer works two to four days a month, runs the KPIs that matter, and costs less than a single patent litigation. The real question is whether you can afford another year without one.

Hayat Amin built the framework Beyond Elevation uses for every new CIPO engagement: the Hayat Amin IP Readiness Scorecard. It scores a company across five dimensions, patent coverage, trade secret hygiene, data licensing readiness, AI IP ownership clarity, and filing roadmap alignment, and produces a single number that tells a board exactly where the IP function stands. A score below 60 means the company is exposed. Below 40 means it is leaving money on the table at every raise and every exit conversation.

The Cost of Waiting

Every month of delay in 2027 IP planning compounds. A patent not filed in January can't establish priority until it's filed. A trade secret that leaks in February can't be un-leaked, and a data asset not catalogued in Q1 won't be ready to license by Q2.

The companies that act in Q4 2026 enter the new year with a defensible, revenue-generating IP position. The ones that wait enter it hoping nobody notices the gaps.

FAQ

What is the most important IP priority for 2027?

AI-generated IP ownership policy. Every company using AI tools is generating output with unclear ownership status, and 2027 regulatory enforcement will punish those without documented policies. Start with a written human-contribution threshold for every AI-assisted output type.

How much does IP strategy planning cost for a small company?

A fractional Chief IP Officer typically costs £3,000 to £8,000 per month for two to four days of work. Four provisional patent filings cost under £12,000 total. The entire 2027 IP priority programme outlined here runs under £30,000 for the year, less than the cost of one patent infringement defence.

When should a company hire a fractional Chief IP Officer?

A company should hire one once it has more than £2 million in revenue, more than one protectable innovation in the pipeline, or sits within 18 months of a fundraise or exit. At that stage, the cost of not having IP leadership exceeds the cost of a fractional engagement by a factor of ten or more.

Can data be licensed as an asset in 2027?

Yes. The data monetisation market is projected to reach $17.6 billion by 2033, according to Grand View Research. Companies with clean provenance documentation, structured datasets, and clear rights ownership are already licensing data to AI companies, research institutions, and industry competitors. The barrier is readiness, not demand.

Does my company need patents if we rely on trade secrets?

Trade secrets and patents do different jobs, and most companies need both. Patents protect innovations you plan to disclose, or that competitors could develop independently. Trade secrets protect information that's valuable because it's secret. The 2026 court rulings on AI tool use make trade secret protection harder, which raises the relative value of patents for anything that can be patented.
