# 70% of AI Projects Fail and the Technology Worked in Every One

AI change management is the structured process of preparing an organisation's people, workflows, and incentives to adopt AI tools that already work. It is the reason 70% of AI projects fail despite the technology performing exactly as advertised. According to McKinsey's 2024 Global AI Survey, 74% of companies reporting failed AI initiatives cite organisational resistance and process misalignment — not model performance — as the primary cause.

Hayat Amin, who has embedded AI operations inside companies from fintech to logistics, argues the diagnosis is even simpler: "The AI worked in the demo. It worked in the pilot. It stopped working the moment it needed a human being to change a habit." Beyond Elevation's AI operations engagements now start with change management before a single tool is configured — because the tool was never the problem.

## What Is AI Change Management and Why Does It Decide Whether AI Ships?

AI change management is the discipline of redesigning workflows, retraining teams, and realigning incentives so that AI tools move from pilot to production. It is not a communications plan or an internal newsletter about "our AI journey." It is the operational work that decides whether the £200,000 you spent on an AI vendor produces a return or produces a case study in what not to do.

Traditional change management — the Kotter model, the ADKAR framework — was built for ERP rollouts and office relocations. AI adoption breaks those models because the change is not a one-time migration. AI tools improve, hallucinate, need monitoring, and reshape job descriptions in ways that an ERP never did. The change management has to be continuous, not phased.

This is why most AI consultancies fail at implementation. They build the demo, hand over documentation, and leave. The organisation reverts within 90 days because nobody rewired the daily habits that the tool was supposed to replace. Hayat Amin calls this the "demo-to-desert" pattern: a working prototype surrounded by people who have no reason to use it.

## Why Do 70% of AI Projects Fail When the Technology Works?

Seventy percent of AI projects fail not because the model was wrong but because the organisation around it did not change. Three forces kill the rollout every time.

**1. The workflow stays the same.** A company buys an AI tool to automate invoice processing. The tool works. But the finance team still routes invoices through the same approval chain, still prints the same cover sheets, and still re-keys the same data into the same spreadsheet. The AI sits on top of a process that was never redesigned to use it. According to Bain's 2025 AI implementation study, 61% of failed AI projects were deployed into unchanged workflows.

**2. The incentives fight the tool.** A sales team is given an AI-powered lead scoring system. The system works. But the sales manager still measures activity by call volume, not by conversion rate. The reps who use the AI make fewer calls and close more deals — and get reprimanded for low activity. The incentive structure punishes the behaviour the tool was built to create.

**3. Nobody owns adoption.** The CTO bought the tool. The IT team deployed it. The department head was "consulted." Nobody wakes up every morning with the job of making sure people actually use it. Gartner's 2025 survey found that only 12% of companies with active AI initiatives have a named individual accountable for AI adoption — not procurement, not development, adoption.

## What Does Hayat Amin's AI Adoption Friction Map Look Like?

Hayat Amin's AI Adoption Friction Map is the diagnostic Beyond Elevation runs before any AI tool goes live. It identifies the five friction points where an AI rollout will stall — and resolves them before a single licence is purchased. The map has five layers, each scored from zero to ten.

**Layer 1 — Process friction.** Does the current workflow need to change before the AI tool can function? If the answer is yes and nobody has redesigned the workflow, the project will fail. Score: how many manual steps must be removed or rerouted before the AI adds value.

**Layer 2 — Incentive friction.** Do the people who must use the tool get rewarded for using it? If the metrics that decide their bonus or their performance review do not change, they will not change. Score: how many KPIs must be updated to align with the new behaviour.

**Layer 3 — Skill friction.** Can the team operate the tool without calling IT? If the answer is no, usage will collapse the moment IT has a backlog. Score: how many training hours stand between the team and independent operation.

**Layer 4 — Data friction.** Is the data the tool needs clean, accessible, and in the right format? Most AI tools fail not because the model is bad but because the data pipeline feeding it was never built. Score: how many data-cleaning or integration steps are required before the tool can run on real inputs.

**Layer 5 — Ownership friction.** Is there a single person whose job title includes the words "AI adoption" or "AI operations"? If not, the project has no owner and will die. Score: binary — zero if no named owner, ten if there is one with a mandate and a budget.

A total friction score above 25 means the organisation is not ready to deploy. Below 15, it can ship. Between 15 and 25, the friction must be resolved first. Hayat Amin argues that running this map saves companies an average of £150,000 in wasted AI licensing and consulting fees — because it stops them buying tools they are structurally incapable of using.

## How Much Does Poor AI Change Management Cost a Company?

Failed AI adoption costs a mid-market company between £250,000 and £1.2 million per failed initiative when you add licensing fees, consulting costs, internal time, and opportunity cost. That number comes from Accenture's 2025 Technology Waste Report, which found that the average enterprise abandoned 3.2 AI projects in the prior 12 months, each costing between $120,000 and $380,000 in direct spend before the plug was pulled.

The indirect cost is worse. Every failed AI project makes the next one harder to fund. Boards become sceptical. Teams become cynical. The phrase "we tried AI and it didn't work" becomes organisational folklore that blocks investment for years. Beyond Elevation's experience across dozens of AI operations engagements shows that the reputational damage of a failed AI rollout inside a company takes 12 to 18 months to reverse — longer than the project itself lasted.

The fix is not to spend less on AI. It is to spend differently. Hayat Amin's position is that for every £1 spent on AI tooling, a company should spend £0.50 on change management. Most companies spend £1 on tooling and £0.00 on change management, then wonder why nobody uses the tool.

## What Should a CEO Do Before Spending Another Pound on AI Tools?

Stop buying tools. Start fixing the organisation. The three steps that separate companies where AI ships from companies where AI stays in pilot forever are all operational, not technical.

**First, appoint a single owner for AI adoption.** Not the CTO. Not the IT manager. A person whose only job is making sure AI tools get used by the people they were built for. This is what a fractional [AI operations](/insights/what-is-ai-operations/) operator does — they sit inside the company, own the adoption, and do not leave after the demo.

**Second, redesign the workflow before deploying the tool.** Map the current process. Identify which steps the AI replaces. Remove those steps. Then deploy. Not the other way around. The [automate-first](/insights/automate-first/) principle applies: the automation is only as good as the process it automates.

**Third, change the metrics.** If the team's performance review does not mention the AI tool, the AI tool does not exist. Align the incentives on day one, not day 90.

AI change management is the process of preparing people, workflows, and incentives to adopt AI tools. It covers everything that happens around the technology — not the technology itself. Without it, AI tools get deployed and ignored.

### Why do most AI projects fail?

Most AI projects fail because the organisation does not change to accommodate the tool. The technology typically works. The workflows, incentives, and ownership structures do not. McKinsey's data shows 74% of AI failures are organisational, not technical.
