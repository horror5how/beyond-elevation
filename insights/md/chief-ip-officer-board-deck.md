## Why Do Most Chief IP Officer Board Decks Fail?

Most IP board decks fail because they speak the language of patent prosecution instead of the language of capital allocation. Board members care about revenue protection, competitive distance, and return on investment. They do not care about Office Action response timelines, IDS filing counts, or the difference between a continuation and a divisional. A Chief IP Officer board deck that leads with claims data instead of commercial outcomes will lose the room in under two minutes.

The failure is structural, not cosmetic. IP teams build decks bottom-up from prosecution activity. Boards think top-down from enterprise value. The 6-slide framework bridges that gap by starting with the only question every board member is actually asking: what is this IP doing for the business?

Hayat Amin's rule for board communication is blunt: &ldquo;If the slide does not answer &lsquo;how much revenue does this protect?&rsquo; or &lsquo;what does this cost us if we lose it?&rsquo;, delete it.&rdquo; Beyond Elevation applies this filter to every Chief IP Officer board deck it builds. The result is consistently the same: the board asks better questions, the CIPO gets a faster budget decision, and the IP function stops being the department nobody understands.

## Slide 1: How Much Revenue Does Your IP Actually Protect?

IP revenue attribution is the single number that earns your board deck a second look. It answers the question every CFO and board member asks silently: what percentage of our revenue depends on IP we own? For most technology companies, the answer falls between 40% and 85% of total revenue — but fewer than 10% of companies actually calculate and present this number. A Chief IP Officer board deck without revenue attribution is a cost report, not a strategy document.

Map every granted patent and active trade secret to the product lines they protect. Assign revenue from those product lines proportionally. Present the number as a single percentage: &ldquo;67% of our &pound;12M revenue is protected by IP we own.&rdquo; Then show the gap — the revenue lines with zero IP coverage. That gap is your budget justification for every new filing you propose.

## Slide 2: What Is Your IP Licensing Pipeline Worth?

Licensing pipeline value turns your patent portfolio from a defensive cost into an offensive revenue line. This slide mirrors the sales pipeline your board already reviews every quarter: prospects, stage, expected value, close date. Most boards have never seen an IP licensing pipeline because most companies do not build one. Creating it is the single fastest way for a Chief IP Officer to change how the board perceives the entire IP function — from cost centre to revenue contributor.

List every licensing opportunity you have identified or are actively pursuing. Include the target licensee industry, the patents in play, the estimated annual royalty value, and the stage of engagement. Even a pipeline with zero closed revenue shifts perception. Hayat Amin showed one client that their 14-patent portfolio had a licensable pipeline worth &pound;2.4M in annual royalties that nobody had pursued. The board approved double the IP budget in the same meeting.

## Slide 3: Where Are Your Competitors Filing Patents?

A competitive filing map shows your board where rivals are investing in IP and where your own coverage has dangerous gaps. This is the slide that creates urgency. When board members see a competitor filing patents in a technology area your company pioneered, the conversation shifts instantly from &ldquo;do we need more patents?&rdquo; to &ldquo;how quickly can we file?&rdquo; A strong competitive map includes the top five competitors by filing volume, their filings grouped by technology cluster, the jurisdictions they are targeting, and the areas where they are accelerating activity year-over-year.

Use patent analytics to build a heat map. Red zones are areas where competitors have dense coverage and you have none. Amber zones are contested. Green zones are your defensive strongholds. One visual tells the board more about competitive risk than 20 pages of patent numbers ever will.

## Slide 4: What Return Are You Getting on Each Patent Filing?

Filing ROI is the metric that separates a strategic Chief IP Officer from a patent filing machine. The average utility patent costs between &pound;8,000 and &pound;15,000 to file and prosecute, plus annual maintenance fees that compound over 20 years. If a patent is not protecting revenue, enabling licensing, or blocking a competitor, it is a pure liability on your books. Filing ROI measures the commercial value generated per pound spent on prosecution — and most portfolios score poorly because they were built without a commercial filter.

Calculate it simply: total IP-attributed value (protected revenue plus licensing revenue) divided by total IP spend (filing, prosecution, maintenance, external counsel). Present the ratio. A healthy portfolio delivers 5:1 or better. Below 2:1, the board is right to question every pound going into the IP function. ## Slide 5: Which Patents Should You Stop Paying For?

Maintenance cost optimisation is the slide that saves money immediately. Every patent requires escalating annual maintenance fees — for a US patent, the fees run roughly $1,600 at year 3.5, $3,600 at year 7.5, and $7,400 at year 11.5. A portfolio of 50 patents costs over &pound;80,000 per year in maintenance alone, and that figure climbs as the portfolio ages. Yet most companies renew every patent automatically, including patents that protect discontinued products, abandoned markets, or technologies the company no longer uses.

Present the board with three buckets: keep (revenue-protecting or strategically critical), review (marginal value, could be licensed or abandoned), and abandon (no commercial connection, costing money for zero return). Hayat Amin's Portfolio Pruning Method typically identifies 15% to 30% of a portfolio as candidates for immediate abandonment — redirecting that maintenance spend toward new filings with actual commercial value. ## Slide 6: What Do You Need the Board to Approve in the Next 90 Days?

The 90-day ask is the slide that converts information into action. Every Chief IP Officer board deck should end with a specific, time-bound request: a filing budget, a licensing campaign authorisation, a portfolio review mandate, or a technology-specific competitive response. The request must include a clear budget figure, an expected outcome with a number attached, and a deadline. Boards approve concrete proposals. They defer vague requests to the next quarter.

Frame the ask against the data in the previous five slides. &ldquo;We have &pound;8.2M in unprotected revenue [Slide 1], three licensing targets worth &pound;1.6M annually [Slide 2], and a competitor filing in our core area at 3x our rate [Slide 3]. I am requesting &pound;120,000 to file four priority patents and initiate two licensing conversations. Expected ROI: 8:1 over 24 months.&rdquo; That is a request a board can approve in the room, not defer to the next meeting.

## FAQ

### How often should a Chief IP Officer present to the board?

Quarterly. IP moves slower than revenue, but faster than most boards realise. A quarterly cadence keeps the board informed on competitive filings, licensing progress, and maintenance decisions without drowning them in operational detail. Between quarters, the CIPO should flag any material competitive threat or licensing opportunity to the board chair directly.

### What if my company does not have a Chief IP Officer?

Most companies under &pound;50M in revenue do not need a full-time CIPO. A fractional Chief IP Officer — typically engaged two to four days per month — builds the same board deck, runs the same portfolio analysis, and drives the same licensing conversations at a fraction of the cost. Beyond Elevation places fractional CIPOs who start delivering board-ready reporting within 30 days.

### Should the IP board deck include patent claim details?

No. Patent claim details belong in the appendix, available if a board member asks. The main deck should contain only commercial data: revenue attribution, pipeline value, competitive positioning, ROI, cost optimisation, and the specific budget request. Claims data in the main deck is the number one reason boards disengage from IP discussions.

### How long should the Chief IP Officer board presentation take?

Fifteen minutes, including questions. If the deck is built correctly — six slides, each with one clear data point and one clear implication — the board gets everything it needs in a quarter of an hour. Hayat Amin's benchmark is direct: if a CIPO needs more than 15 minutes, the deck has too many slides or the wrong data on them.
