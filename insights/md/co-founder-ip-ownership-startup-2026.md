# Who Really Owns Your Startup's IP? The Co-Founder Ownership Gap That Kills Deals

One in three startup acquisitions that collapse during due diligence fail because of co-founder IP ownership disputes. Not market risk. Not revenue shortfalls. IP ownership — the question nobody asked when the company was two people in a garage.

Hayat Amin has seen this pattern destroy more deals than cap table disputes and revenue misses combined. "Founders split equity on day one," Hayat Amin argues, "but nobody splits the IP. Then a co-founder leaves, and suddenly the company's most valuable assets are owned by someone who is suing you." This is the co-founder IP ownership gap — and if you have not addressed it, your next fundraise or exit is at risk.

## Who Owns IP Created Before the Company Exists?

IP created before incorporation belongs to the individual who created it — not the company — unless a written agreement explicitly assigns it. This default rule catches founders off guard because they assume forming a company automatically transfers their prior work into it. It does not.

Pre-incorporation IP — code written before the LLC filing, algorithms prototyped in a university lab, datasets assembled during a consulting engagement — remains the personal property of whoever created it. When that person is a co-founder, the company has a dependency on an asset it does not own.

The fix is a **background IP declaration**: a written list of every piece of pre-existing IP each co-founder brings to the venture, with a corresponding assignment or licence agreement. Beyond Elevation runs this exercise with every new client during the first week of engagement. Without it, a single investor question during due diligence — "who actually owns the codebase?" — can stall a round for months.

Hayat Amin's rule is blunt: "If you cannot point to a signed assignment for every line of code that predates your incorporation, you do not have a company — you have a licensing dependency on your co-founder's goodwill."

## What Happens to Co-Founder IP Ownership When Someone Leaves?

When a co-founder departs without a properly executed IP assignment agreement, they retain a legal claim to every patent, trade secret, and piece of code they contributed. The company's ability to license, sell, or enforce that IP is compromised until the ownership question is resolved — usually through expensive litigation or a settlement that transfers value out of the company.

The scenario plays out in predictable stages. The departing co-founder does not immediately assert ownership. Six to eighteen months pass. Then the company raises a round or enters acquisition talks. The departing co-founder's lawyer sends a letter. The deal reprices or dies.

The preventive measure is an IP assignment agreement signed at formation that includes a **survival clause** — language ensuring IP assignments remain in effect regardless of the co-founder's employment status, equity vesting, or relationship with the company. Companies with patents are 10.2x more likely to secure early-stage funding, but only if ownership of those patents is unambiguous.

## What Should a Co-Founder IP Ownership Agreement Include?

A proper co-founder IP agreement must cover seven distinct elements to survive investor scrutiny, acquirer due diligence, and a departing co-founder's legal challenge. Hayat Amin developed the **Co-Founder IP Assignment Protocol** after seeing the same seven gaps repeat across hundreds of startup IP audits at Beyond Elevation.

**1. Background IP schedule.** Every co-founder lists all pre-existing IP they are contributing. This becomes Exhibit A in every future IP dispute.

**2. Forward assignment clause.** All IP created by any co-founder in connection with the company's business — during work hours or not — is automatically assigned to the company. "In connection with" is broader than "during work hours" and closes the side-project loophole that founders routinely exploit.

**3. Moral rights waiver.** In jurisdictions that recognise moral rights (UK, EU, most of the world outside the US), co-founders must waive the right to be identified as the author or to object to modifications of their work.

**4. Survival clause.** IP assignments survive termination of employment, termination of the co-founder relationship, and expiration of equity vesting. Without this clause, a departing co-founder can argue the assignment expired when they left.

**5. Cooperation obligation.** Co-founders agree to sign any future documents required to perfect IP assignments — patent assignment recordals, copyright registrations, trade secret declarations. This eliminates the leverage a departing co-founder holds when the company needs their signature on a filing.

**6. Non-compete IP scope.** A narrowly defined non-compete that prevents a departing co-founder from developing competing IP for a specified period. Too broad and it is unenforceable. Too narrow and it provides no protection.

**7. Dispute resolution mechanism.** Arbitration, not litigation. Startup co-founder disputes that enter public litigation destroy enterprise value faster than the underlying IP issue warrants.

## How Does Co-Founder IP Ownership Affect Startup Valuation?

Unclear co-founder IP ownership directly reduces startup valuation by 20 to 40 percent in acquisition scenarios and causes fundraising rounds to reprice or fail. Investors and acquirers apply a discount to any company where IP ownership is contested, ambiguous, or dependent on undocumented verbal agreements between co-founders.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that investors price defensibility, not ideas and not growth. "If your co-founder can walk out the door and claim half your patent portfolio, you have zero defensibility. The investor knows it. The acquirer's lawyer will find it. And the valuation reflects it."

The premium works in reverse too. Companies that present clean IP assignment chains during due diligence — signed agreements, background IP schedules, recorded patent assignments — consistently close at higher multiples. Beyond Elevation's research shows that companies with documented IP ownership command a 41 percent higher enterprise-value multiple than comparable companies with IP assignment gaps.

The practical implication: fixing co-founder IP ownership before a fundraise or exit is not a legal housekeeping task — it is a valuation driver that directly impacts your multiple.

## How Do You Fix a Co-Founder IP Assignment Gap After the Fact?

Fixing a co-founder IP assignment gap after formation requires a retroactive assignment agreement, executed before any external event — fundraise, partnership, or acquisition — forces the issue. The longer the gap persists, the more leverage the unassigned co-founder accumulates and the higher the cost of resolution.

**Step 1: Inventory the gap.** Map every piece of IP to its creator. Identify which assets have written assignments and which do not. Hayat Amin calls this the "IP chain of title" exercise — the same due diligence an acquirer would run, except you are running it on yourself before they do.

**Step 2: Negotiate the retroactive assignment.** Approach the co-founder — active or departed — with a specific assignment proposal. If they are still active, the assignment is typically executed as part of an updated operating agreement. If they have departed, the negotiation is more complex and may require consideration — cash, equity adjustments, or royalty participation — to secure their signature.

**Step 3: Record the assignments.** File patent assignment recordals with the relevant patent office (USPTO, EPO, UKIPO). Update copyright registrations. Document trade secret provenance. A signed agreement without recorded assignments is a handshake, not a perfected transfer.

**Step 4: Issue representations.** Once assignments are recorded, issue formal IP representations in your next investor update or board resolution. This creates a timestamped record that the gap has been closed, which satisfies future due diligence inquiries.

The cost of fixing a co-founder IP gap early is measured in thousands of dollars of legal fees. The cost of fixing it during an acquisition is measured in millions of dollars of repriced deal value — or the deal itself. Start with a full IP strategy review before your next fundraise.

## FAQ

### Can a co-founder claim IP ownership if there is no written agreement?

Yes. Without a written IP assignment agreement, the default legal rule in most jurisdictions is that the creator owns the IP they created. A co-founder who wrote code, designed a system, or invented a process owns that work product personally — not the company — unless a signed agreement says otherwise. Verbal agreements about IP are difficult to prove and rarely survive litigation.

### Should co-founders file patents jointly or under the company?

Patents should always be filed under the company, not in the names of individual co-founders. Joint inventorship is separate from ownership — co-founders can be listed as named inventors while assigning all ownership rights to the company entity. Filing in individual names creates a co-ownership situation that limits the company's ability to license or enforce the patent without each co-owner's consent.

### How does co-founder IP ownership work with equity vesting?

IP ownership and equity vesting should be completely independent. A co-founder's IP assignment must survive regardless of how much equity has vested. If a co-founder departs at 25 percent vesting, they forfeit 75 percent of their equity — but the IP they created should remain 100 percent with the company. Linking IP ownership to vesting creates a structural error: the less equity someone has when they leave, the more incentive they have to assert IP claims as alternative leverage.

### What if a co-founder refuses to sign an IP assignment agreement?

A co-founder who refuses to sign an IP assignment is signalling that they do not trust the partnership or that they are preserving optionality to leave and compete. The conversation should happen at formation, when leverage is equal. If it happens later, the company may need to offer consideration — a signing bonus, accelerated vesting, or a retroactive licence fee — to secure the signature. Do not proceed with fundraising or customer contracts until every co-founder has signed.

### Does a co-founder IP agreement replace a standard employment contract?

No. A co-founder IP agreement supplements an employment or service agreement — it does not replace it. Co-founders who are also employees need both: an employment contract governing role, compensation, and termination, and a separate IP assignment covering pre-existing IP, forward-created IP, moral rights waivers, survival clauses, and cooperation obligations. The IP assignment should be a standalone document that survives the employment relationship.
