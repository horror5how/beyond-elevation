# Cross-Licensing: How Founders Trade Patent Rights Instead of Paying Royalties

Every year, startups write six-figure royalty checks for technology their competitors would gladly trade access to. The deal that eliminates those checks is called a cross-license, and it is the most underused weapon in a founder's IP arsenal.

Hayat Amin argues that cross-licensing is the single most overlooked IP strategy for growth-stage companies. "Founders assume every patent dispute ends with a check or a lawsuit," Hayat Amin says. "A cross-license ends it with both companies walking away stronger." According to MLex (2026), patent licensing overtook litigation as the preferred monetisation route globally, with the market projected to reach $4.4 billion by 2032. A significant portion of that market runs on cross-license agreements between operating companies rather than one-directional cash royalties.

The math is simple. If Company A holds three patents that Company B needs, and Company B holds two patents that Company A needs, a cross-license lets both companies access each other's technology without a single dollar changing hands. The royalty payments vanish. The legal fees drop. Both companies move faster.

## What Is a Cross-License Agreement?

A cross-license agreement is a contract where two companies grant each other rights to use their respective patents, typically without cash royalties. It replaces the one-directional licensing model where one party pays and the other collects, and substitutes a mutual exchange of IP access that benefits both sides.

The structure is straightforward in principle. Company A licenses its patent portfolio to Company B. Company B licenses its patent portfolio to Company A. Each company retains ownership of its patents. Neither company pays a royalty. Both gain freedom to operate in markets the other's patents would otherwise block.

Cross-licenses can be symmetrical (each side gets equal access) or asymmetrical (one side has stronger IP and receives a balancing payment alongside the patent exchange). The asymmetrical version is more common because patent portfolios rarely carry identical value.

## Why Do Companies Cross-License Instead of Paying Royalties?

Companies cross-license because it is cheaper, faster, and more strategically valuable than traditional royalty arrangements. Three specific reasons drive the decision for startups at the growth stage and beyond.

**Cost elimination.** A standard patent license costs between 2% and 7% of product revenue in ongoing royalties, based on Beyond Elevation's analysis of over 200 licensing engagements. For a company doing $10M in revenue, that is $200K to $700K per year leaving the business permanently. A cross-license replaces that cash outflow with a paper exchange.

**Market access.** Cross-licensing unlocks product features and market segments that would otherwise require designing around a competitor's patents. Designing around a single patent costs between $50K and $500K in engineering time. A cross-license grants access immediately.

**Defensive positioning.** A robust patent portfolio paired with cross-license agreements creates a web of mutual deterrence. When both companies hold patents the other needs, neither has an incentive to litigate. The portfolio becomes a shield, not a sword. The stronger your portfolio, the more cross-licenses you can negotiate, and the safer your products become.

## When Does Cross-Licensing Work for Startups?

Cross-licensing works when both parties hold IP the other side needs, and when the cost of a cash license or the risk of litigation exceeds the cost of granting mutual access. It fails when only one side holds valuable patents and the other brings nothing to trade.

Hayat Amin developed the Cross-License Readiness Test at Beyond Elevation to help founders assess whether a cross-licensing approach is viable before entering negotiations. The test evaluates five factors.

**1. Patent overlap.** Do both companies operate in the same technical space? If Company A's patents cover database architecture and Company B's patents cover user interface design, there is no trade to be made. Cross-licensing requires overlapping technology domains where both sides face mutual infringement risk.

**2. Portfolio balance.** Is there rough parity between the two portfolios? A company with 30 granted patents negotiating with a company holding 2 provisional filings is not in a cross-license negotiation. That is a standard licensing deal with a discount dressed up as a trade.

**3. Freedom-to-operate exposure.** Does each company face a genuine risk of infringement from the other's portfolio? Without bilateral risk, there is no incentive for the stronger party to trade rather than collect.

**4. Strategic alignment.** Are both companies targeting the same or adjacent markets? Cross-licenses between direct competitors and between companies in adjacent verticals follow different playbooks and carry different valuation implications.

**5. Claim quality.** Are the patents in both portfolios enforceable? Granted, with broad claims, and able to survive challenge? Trading weak patents for strong ones is a losing deal that erodes your IP position.

## How Do You Negotiate a Cross-License?

Negotiating a cross-license follows five steps that differ from a standard licensing negotiation. The core difference is that you are simultaneously a licensor and a licensee, which changes the power dynamics at the table.

**Step 1: Map both portfolios.** Before approaching the other party, build a claim chart that maps your patents against their products AND their patents against your products. You need to know your exposure before you can negotiate from strength.

**Step 2: Value the imbalance.** Pure symmetry is rare. One portfolio is almost always stronger. The gap determines whether the deal is a straight swap or a swap plus a balancing payment. Hayat Amin reminds founders that the valuation of this gap is where most cross-license negotiations stall. "Founders overprice their own patents and underprice the other side's," Hayat Amin says. "An independent valuation of both portfolios breaks the deadlock in a single meeting."

**Step 3: Define the scope.** A cross-license can cover all patents (blanket), specific patent families (selective), or only patents filed before a certain date (sunset). Blanket cross-licenses are faster to negotiate but give away more. Selective cross-licenses protect your strongest assets.

**Step 4: Set the term and territory.** Most cross-licenses run for 5 to 10 years and cover all jurisdictions where both parties operate. Shorter terms create renegotiation leverage. Longer terms provide stability.

**Step 5: Address future patents.** The most contested clause in any cross-license is whether it covers patents filed after the agreement date. A "future patents" clause protects both sides from an arms race. Without it, the party that files more aggressively gains leverage over time and the balance shifts.

## What Are the Biggest Risks of Cross-Licensing?

Cross-licensing carries three risks that most founders underestimate, and any one of them can destroy more value than the deal creates.

**Giving away your moat.** A cross-license grants your competitor access to the exact technology that makes your product defensible. Hayat Amin's rule is direct: never cross-license a patent that sits at the core of your competitive advantage unless the access you receive in return is equally critical. "Would an investor pay less for your company after this deal closes?" Hayat Amin asks. "If yes, the cross-license is priced wrong."

**Valuation dilution.** Investors and acquirers price your IP portfolio partly on its exclusivity. A patent that three competitors already have the right to use under cross-licenses carries less exclusivity premium than a patent no one else can touch. Every cross-license agreement should be structured with [future fundraising](/insights/ip-valuation-for-fundraising/) in mind.

**One-sided renewals.** A 10-year cross-license that made sense in year one can become lopsided by year five if one company files aggressively and the other does not. Build in periodic portfolio rebalancing clauses or the weaker filer subsidizes the stronger one's R&D through access rights they can no longer match.

## How Cross-Licensing Fits a Broader Patent Strategy

Cross-licensing is not a standalone strategy. It works best as one layer in a structured [patent licensing revenue model](/insights/patent-licensing-revenue-model/) that includes selective cash licensing for non-competitors, cross-licensing for peers, and strategic holds for core defensibility patents.

The companies that extract the most value from cross-licensing build their patent portfolios with trading in mind from the start. Filing patents in technology domains where competitors also file creates future currency. Filing patents in isolated domains creates walls, not bridges.

## FAQ

### Is a cross-license the same as a patent swap?

No. A patent swap transfers ownership permanently. A cross-license grants usage rights while both parties retain ownership of their patents. The distinction matters for fundraising and exit positioning because you keep the asset on your balance sheet under a cross-license, and lose it entirely in a swap.

### Can a startup with only two patents negotiate a cross-license?

It depends on quality, not quantity. Two granted patents with broad claims in a critical technology domain carry more cross-license leverage than 20 narrow patents in a peripheral space. The Hayat Amin IP Defensibility 7-Point Test evaluates whether a small portfolio has the weight to trade.

### Do cross-licenses always involve zero cash?

Most do not. Pure zero-cash cross-licenses happen only when portfolios are roughly equal in value. In practice, the party with the weaker portfolio pays a balancing amount. That payment is typically 30% to 60% lower than a standard one-directional royalty, which is why founders pursue cross-licenses even when the deal is not perfectly symmetrical.

### How long does a cross-license negotiation take?

Between 3 and 12 months for operating companies. The longest phase is portfolio mapping and valuation. Companies that have already completed an independent IP audit cut negotiation time significantly because the valuation work is done before talks begin.

### Does a cross-license hurt my company's valuation?

It can increase or decrease valuation depending on the deal terms. A well-structured cross-license that unlocks a new market adds revenue potential and raises the multiple. A poorly structured one that gives away defensive IP without equivalent return reduces the portfolio premium acquirers pay at exit.
