# Fractional CFO Cash Management: The 60-Day Fix for Companies Growing Into a Cash Crisis

82 percent of startup failures involve cash flow problems, not revenue problems. According to a 2023 CB Insights analysis, companies mostly die because they run out of money before the revenue they have turns into cash they can spend, not because nobody wants the product. Hayat Amin, who has sat as fractional CFO across three prior exits including American Express and TripAdvisor acquisitions, argues that most founders confuse a profit-and-loss statement with a bank statement, and that confusion kills companies faster than bad product-market fit.

Fractional CFO cash management is the discipline of converting revenue into usable cash on a predictable, measurable cadence. It's a 60-day operational overhaul, not bookkeeping or quarterly reporting, that changes how cash enters, sits in and leaves your business, run by an operator who has done it before.

## What is fractional CFO cash management?

Fractional CFO cash management is the process of building a cash operating system inside a company that has outgrown its bank balance as a planning tool. A fractional CFO installs cash visibility, cash controls, and cash forecasting so that the founder never discovers a cash crisis by checking the bank app on a Sunday night. This is the single highest-ROI engagement Beyond Elevation runs for growth-stage companies.

Most startups track revenue. Almost none track cash conversion, the gap between when revenue is recognised and when cash arrives. That gap is where companies die. A SaaS business with $3 million in annual recurring revenue can be 60 days from payroll failure if its collection cycle runs 75 days and its vendor payments run 30. The P&L looks healthy. The bank account does not.

A fractional CFO closes this gap by building the systems that make cash visible, predictable, and controlled. The work is operational, not advisory. It involves touching invoices, payment terms, vendor contracts, payroll timing, and treasury policy, not writing a strategy deck.

## Why do growing companies run out of cash?

Growing companies run out of cash because growth consumes cash before it generates it. Every new customer costs money to acquire, onboard, and serve before they pay. Every new hire draws salary before they produce revenue. Every new market requires upfront spend with delayed returns. The faster a company grows, the wider this cash-timing gap becomes, and the more dangerous it gets without active fractional CFO cash management in place.

Three specific patterns cause most startup cash crises:

**Collection lag.** Revenue is booked on the day the invoice is sent. Cash arrives 45, 60, or 90 days later. If the company hires against booked revenue rather than collected cash, it is spending money it does not yet have. Hayat Amin calls this the "revenue hallucination": the P&L says the company is growing at 40 percent while the bank balance is shrinking.

**Margin erosion at scale.** Unit economics that work at $500K in revenue break at $2 million. Hosting costs scale non-linearly. Support headcount outpaces revenue. Discounting creeps in as the sales team chases larger deals. Without a CFO tracking gross margin by product line and customer cohort, the erosion is invisible until the cash is gone.

**Capital expenditure without cash modelling.** A company approves a $400K platform migration, a $200K office buildout, or a $150K compliance programme without modelling the cash impact across the next 13 weeks. The annual budget says it fits. The weekly cash position says it does not.

## What does a fractional CFO fix in the first 60 days?

A fractional CFO builds a cash operating system in 60 days by running five interventions in sequence. This is what Hayat Amin's 60-Day Cash Clarity Framework delivers: a structured sprint from zero cash visibility to a fully operational forecasting and control system. The order matters. Each intervention produces the data the next one needs. Skip a step and the system collapses back into guesswork within a quarter.

**Week 1 to 2: Cash visibility.** Build a 13-week rolling cash forecast. This is the single most important financial tool a growth company can have, more important than the annual budget, the board pack, or the monthly management accounts. It maps every expected cash inflow and outflow across 91 days at weekly granularity. Beyond Elevation's fractional CFOs build this in the first five working days. For a detailed walkthrough, read the guide on [the 13-week cash flow model](/insights/cash-flow-forecast-13-week-model/).

**Week 3 to 4: Collection acceleration.** Audit every outstanding receivable. Tighten payment terms on new contracts from net-60 to net-30 where possible. Implement automated invoice reminders at day 7, day 21, and day 28. Assign collection responsibility to a named person, not the founder, not the sales team. This single intervention typically accelerates cash inflow by 15 to 25 percent within 30 days.

**Week 4 to 5: Payment timing optimisation.** Review every vendor payment schedule. Check whether early-payment discounts justify the cash impact, and drop the ones that don't. For everything else, extend payment terms to match your collection cycles. The goal is to narrow the gap between when cash comes in and when cash goes out, the cash conversion cycle.

**Week 5 to 7: Expense controls.** Install approval thresholds. No spend above a defined limit without finance sign-off. Review every recurring subscription and contractor payment against actual usage. The average startup carries 12 to 18 percent in unused or underused SaaS subscriptions. That is cash recovered in a single afternoon's audit.

**Week 7 to 8: Scenario modelling.** Build three scenarios, base case, downside, and stress, against the 13-week forecast. The base case is what happens if nothing changes. The downside is what happens if one major customer delays payment by 30 days. The stress case is what happens if revenue drops 20 percent for two consecutive months. The framework gives visibility into where cash is today, and a decision engine for what to do when it moves.

## How does fractional CFO cash management differ from financial reporting?

Cash management is an operational discipline that tells you what will happen. Financial reporting is a compliance exercise that tells you what already happened. They share data but serve entirely different purposes, and treating them as the same function is the mistake that leaves founders surprised by empty bank accounts despite healthy-looking management accounts.

A set of management accounts, delivered 15 days after month-end, says last month's revenue was $280K and expenses were $240K. It does not say that $180K of that revenue remains uncollected, that $120K in vendor payments are due next Tuesday, and that payroll on the 25th requires $95K that is not currently in the account.

Hayat Amin argues the first question every founder should ask their finance function each Monday morning is how many weeks of cash runway remain at current burn, not what last month's numbers were. If the finance function cannot answer that question instantly, the company does not have fractional CFO cash management. It has bookkeeping with a senior title.

## When should a company hire a fractional CFO for cash management?

A company needs a fractional CFO for cash management when any three of these five signals appear at the same time. One signal is a data point. Two is a pattern. Three is a system failure that will not fix itself. For a deeper look at the full list of triggers, read the guide on [when to hire a fractional CFO](/insights/when-to-hire-fractional-cfo/).

**Signal 1:** The founder checks the bank balance more than twice a week to decide whether to approve a spend. That's anxiety masquerading as oversight, not financial management.

**Signal 2:** The company has missed or nearly missed payroll, a vendor payment, or a tax deadline in the last 12 months.

**Signal 3:** There is no written cash forecast. Revenue projections exist. Cash projections do not.

**Signal 4:** Accounts receivable days outstanding exceed 50 and nobody is tracking the number.

**Signal 5:** The company raised capital in the last 18 months and cannot answer how many months of runway remain at current burn without opening a spreadsheet.

If three of these five describe your company, the cost of a fractional CFO, typically £3,000 to £7,000 per month for a two-day-a-week engagement, is a fraction of the cost of the cash crisis that is coming.

## FAQ

### What is fractional CFO cash management?

Fractional CFO cash management is the practice of hiring a CFO for a few days a week, specifically to build cash visibility, cash controls, and cash forecasting systems inside a growing company. The fractional CFO installs a cash operating system, typically centred on a 13-week rolling forecast, that makes cash position predictable and manageable rather than reactive.

### How fast can a fractional CFO fix a cash problem?

A structured cash management engagement delivers results in 60 days. Cash visibility arrives in the first two weeks through a 13-week forecast. Collection acceleration and expense controls follow over weeks three through six. By week eight, the company has a functioning cash operating system with scenario models that prevent future surprises.

### Can a bookkeeper handle cash management instead of a CFO?

A bookkeeper records transactions. A CFO manages cash. The difference is the same as a thermometer versus a thermostat. A bookkeeper tells you the bank balance. A fractional CFO forecasts it, controls it, and intervenes before it becomes a crisis. If your company's revenue exceeds £500K and you rely on a bookkeeper for cash decisions, the gap is costing you money every month.

### What is the first thing a fractional CFO does for cash management?

The first deliverable is always a 13-week rolling cash forecast. It maps every expected cash inflow and outflow across 91 days at weekly granularity. This forecast replaces the bank-balance check as the company's primary cash planning tool and becomes the foundation for every subsequent cash management intervention.
