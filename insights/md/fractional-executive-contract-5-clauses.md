# 5 Clauses in Your Fractional Executive Contract That Separate an Operator From an Expensive Advisor

The average fractional executive engagement runs $15,000 to $25,000 a month. According to a 2026 Fractional Executive Association survey, 67 percent of founders who called their fractional hire "disappointing" blamed the contract's scope, not the person they'd hired. Five clauses decide whether you get an operator who builds a function, or an advisor who attends meetings and sends slide decks. Hayat Amin has structured fractional engagements as an operator across three continents, and argues the contract is where most fractional relationships are won or lost, before the first week even starts.

A fractional executive contract isn't a scaled-down employment agreement. It's a scoping document that defines what gets built, who owns which decisions, and what the company looks like once the operator leaves. Get these five clauses right and the engagement pays for itself inside 90 days. Get them wrong and you're paying C-suite rates for someone who drops in a few days a month and advises from a distance.

## What should a fractional executive contract scope clause define?

The scope clause should list three to five deliverables with measurable outcomes and deadlines, not a paragraph of responsibilities. A scope built around deliverables forces the fractional executive to build something durable, a finance function, an IP filing roadmap, an AI operations stack, rather than advise on it from a distance. Word it that way and the results follow.

Most founders take a permanent job description and just cut the hours, swapping five days for two. That's where the disappointment starts. A permanent CFO's job description says "oversee financial planning and analysis." A fractional contract with the same wording gets you someone who turns up, reviews spreadsheets, and leaves. Hayat Amin's rule is blunt: if your scope reads like a job description, you've hired an employee at contractor rates. If it reads like a project charter with milestones, you've hired an operator.

The scope clause should answer three questions. What does the function look like at the end of month three? What decisions will this person make without asking permission? What does "done" look like, the point at which the function runs without them?

Example of a weak scope: "Provide strategic financial oversight and advise the CEO on capital allocation." Example of an operator scope: "Deliver a board pack ready for investors by week six. Close the books in under five business days by month three. Build and hire a two-person finance team by month four."

## How much decision authority should a fractional executive contract grant?

A fractional executive needs signing authority up to a defined threshold, typically $5,000 to $50,000 depending on company stage, without requiring CEO or board approval on every transaction. Without this, every operational decision loops back through you, and you're paying operator rates for someone who works as a coordinator waiting on approvals.

The authority clause is where most fractional executive contracts fail silently. The founder hires a fractional CFO to "run finance" but keeps approval over every vendor payment, every hire, every contract above $2,000. The fractional executive spends 40 percent of their limited time chasing sign-offs instead of building the function.

Hayat Amin's **Fractional Authority Matrix**, the framework [Beyond Elevation](https://beyondelevation.com) uses to scope every operator engagement, maps authority to three tiers. Tier one is operational: expenses, vendor renewals, and process changes below the threshold, no approval needed. Tier two is tactical: hires, system changes, and budget reallocation above the threshold, CEO approval within 48 hours. Tier three is strategic: equity commitments, M&A positioning, and board-level decisions, board approval required. The matrix goes into the contract as an appendix, so both sides know exactly where the line sits before day one.

## What reporting cadence works for a fractional engagement?

Weekly asynchronous written updates plus a fortnightly 30-minute call works for most company stages. Monthly reporting is too slow, problems compound for three weeks before anyone sees them. Daily standups are too noisy, you hired a fractional executive to free up your time, not fill it with another recurring meeting.

The reporting clause matters because a fractional executive isn't in the office every day. Without a defined cadence, communication defaults to Slack messages and ad hoc calls that neither side controls. The contract should specify the format (a written memo, not a slide deck), the frequency, and the escalation path for issues that can't wait until the next scheduled update.

The written update is non-negotiable. A fractional executive who only reports verbally leaves no institutional memory. If the engagement ends, everything they observed, decided, and recommended disappears with them. A weekly written memo builds a searchable archive that the next hire, fractional or permanent, can read on day one.

## What exit terms should a fractional executive contract include?

A 90-day notice period with a documented 30-day handoff is the standard that protects the company without trapping the operator in an engagement past its usefulness. The fractional executive contract should require the departing operator to leave behind documented standard operating procedures, at least one trained internal person, and a 90-day forward playbook covering what happens next.

Hayat Amin tells the story of a SaaS founder who renewed a fractional CFO engagement for 18 months because the contract had no transition clause. The CFO had built the function around themselves, every process required their involvement, every vendor relationship sat in their inbox, every board question routed through them. The founder couldn't afford to let them go, because nothing would work without them. It had stopped being a fractional engagement and turned into a permanent dependency billed as one.

The exit clause should include three components. First, a transition milestone: the fractional executive documents every process they own by month two, not on their way out. Second, a successor plan: who inside the company takes over each responsibility, and what training they get during the engagement. Third, a clean break: all company data, credentials and vendor access return to the company within five business days of the last day.

## What should a fractional executive contract cost, and what are the pricing red flags?

Monthly retainers for a fractional executive range from $8,000 for a two-day-a-week junior fractional to $35,000 for a three-day-a-week C-suite operator with exit experience. Any engagement priced purely by the hour is a consulting arrangement dressed up as a fractional role, and it misaligns incentives, because the operator earns more by working slower.

The pricing clause should set a fixed monthly retainer tied to the scope deliverables, not to hours logged. A retainer creates alignment: the operator is motivated to build the function efficiently because their pay doesn't change whether a task takes two hours or ten. Hourly billing pushes the opposite incentive.

Red flags in pricing: if a "fractional" engagement for two days a week costs more than $40,000 a month, you're paying a full-time salary for two days of attention, so just hire someone full-time. A retainer below $6,000 a month for a C-suite title usually means you've hired someone testing the fractional model, not a proven operator. Any engagement that bundles the retainer with "success fees" or "equity in lieu of cash" is really a consulting arrangement that puts the operator's financial interests ahead of your function.

## The clause most founders forget: non-compete and IP assignment

A fractional executive serves multiple clients. Your contract must address what they can and cannot do with the knowledge they gain inside your company. A blanket non-compete is unenforceable for a fractional executive in most jurisdictions and will scare away the best operators. A targeted confidentiality and IP assignment clause is both enforceable and necessary.

The clause should specify that all work product created during the engagement belongs to the company. It should include a named list of direct competitors the operator agrees not to serve concurrently. And it should require the operator to disclose any conflicts of interest before the engagement starts and as they arise. Beyond Elevation builds this clause into every operator engagement, because discovering a conflict six months in is expensive and avoidable.

Hayat Amin says the IP assignment clause is the one most founders skip, and the one that costs the most when it's missing. A fractional CFO who builds your financial model, your board pack template and your investor data room has created company IP. Without an assignment clause, ownership of that work product is legally ambiguous, and that ambiguity is the fastest way to lose value in a [due diligence process](/insights/ip-due-diligence-ma-guide/).

## FAQ

### what is a fractional executive contract

A fractional executive contract is a scoping agreement between a company and a part-time C-suite operator. It sets out deliverables, decision authority, reporting cadence, pricing and transition terms. It differs from an employment agreement because it's based on deliverables, not on being present, and from a consulting agreement because it grants operational authority rather than advisory access.

### how long should a fractional executive engagement last

Most fractional executive engagements run six to twelve months with a 90 day review at the halfway point. The engagement should end when the function the operator was hired to build runs without them, not when a calendar date arrives. Engagements that run beyond 18 months without a clear end state usually mean the contract lacked transition milestones.

### can a fractional executive work for competitors at the same time

A fractional executive typically serves three to five clients at once. That's how the model works economically. The contract should name specific direct competitors the operator agrees not to serve concurrently, rather than impose a blanket industry non-compete that would make the engagement unworkable. Confidentiality clauses, not non-competes, are the main protection.

### should a fractional executive contract include equity

Equity should sit alongside a fair retainer, not replace one. A fractional executive who accepts below-market cash in exchange for equity has a [different incentive structure](/insights/fractional-executive-cost-comparison/) than one paid a market retainer. If you offer equity, vest it monthly over the engagement term with no cliff. Fractional operators prove their value in weeks, not years.

### how is a fractional executive different from a management consultant

A fractional executive has decision authority within the company and is accountable for operational outcomes. A management consultant advises and recommends but doesn't own decisions or results. The contract reflects this: a fractional executive contract includes signing authority thresholds, direct reports and system access. A consulting agreement typically includes none of these.
