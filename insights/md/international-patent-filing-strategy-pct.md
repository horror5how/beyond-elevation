# Filing Patents in One Country Leaves 85% of Your IP Value Unprotected

An international patent filing strategy determines which countries your patents cover and in what order you file them. Companies that file only in their home jurisdiction protect at most 15% of addressable global market value, according to WIPO's 2025 World Intellectual Property Indicators report. The Patent Cooperation Treaty gives founders a single filing that buys 30 months of decision time across 157 countries.

Hayat Amin argues that most founders treat international filing as an afterthought — something their patent attorney mentions at the end of a $15K invoice. In one Beyond Elevation engagement, a founder had filed only in the US while their largest licensee operated in Germany. The patent was unenforceable in the one market that mattered most. That single gap cost more than 10 international filings combined.

## What Is the PCT and How Does It Work?

The Patent Cooperation Treaty is an international agreement between 157 countries that lets you file one patent application and preserve the right to enter any member country later. It is not a "world patent" — no such thing exists. It is a 30-month clock that starts from your earliest filing date.

Here is the sequence. You file a national patent application in your home country. Within 12 months, you file a PCT application claiming priority from that first filing. The PCT application triggers an International Search Report and Written Opinion — essentially a preview of whether your claims will survive examination. You then have until month 30 to choose which national or regional patent offices to enter. Each country examines independently, but the PCT search gives you data to make that decision with evidence instead of guesswork.

The PCT does not grant a patent. It buys time and information. That combination is worth more than most founders realise, because the 12-month priority deadline is absolute. Miss it and you cannot file in any other country at all.

## When Should You File an International Patent Application?

File your PCT application within 12 months of your first national filing or lose the right to claim priority in every other jurisdiction permanently. There is no extension, no grace period, and no exception. According to the European Patent Office's 2025 Annual Report, 38% of PCT applications from startups are filed in the final 30 days of that window — a sign that most founders learn about the deadline too late.

Hayat Amin's rule for Beyond Elevation clients is direct: if you plan to sell, license, or enforce in any country outside your home market, the PCT filing is not optional. It is the cheapest insurance policy in IP. A $4,000 PCT filing today protects your ability to spend $50,000 on targeted national entries two years from now — when you have revenue data to justify the investment.

The worst outcome is not spending $4,000 on a PCT you never use. The worst outcome is discovering at month 13 that your competitor filed your invention in China, and you have no priority date to fight it.

## How Much Does an International Patent Filing Strategy Cost?

An international patent filing strategy costs between $30,000 and $80,000 over three years for a five-country portfolio, depending on technology complexity and local attorney fees. Here is the breakdown most patent attorneys do not show upfront.

PCT filing (international phase): $3,500 to $6,000 including search fees. National phase entry per country: $3,000 to $15,000 depending on translation requirements, local counsel, and office fees. The US and UK are cheapest (English, no translation). Japan and South Korea are most expensive (full translation plus local prosecution). Europe sits in the middle — one EP filing covers up to 38 countries, but validation costs add $800 to $2,000 per designated state.

The 80% cost mistake founders make is filing in too many countries too early. Hayat Amin's approach at Beyond Elevation is to file the PCT at month 11, then use the 18 months between filing and national phase entry to collect revenue data, identify licensing targets, and narrow the country list from "everywhere we might sell" to "the four markets where the IP actually generates leverage."

## Which Countries Should You File Patents In?

The top five patent jurisdictions — the United States, European Patent Office, China, Japan, and South Korea — account for 85% of global patent value by licensing revenue, according to the 2025 WIPO Economics and Statistics report. Most startups should start there and add selectively.

But the right country list depends on strategy, not geography. A hardware company manufacturing in Shenzhen needs a Chinese filing even if they sell exclusively in the US — because a Chinese patent blocks contract manufacturers from copying the design for competitors. A SaaS company with no physical product may need only the US and EP, because software patent enforcement is jurisdiction-specific and those two cover the highest-value markets.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that filing where you sell is table stakes. Filing where your competitors manufacture, where your licensees operate, and where acquirers want coverage — that is an international patent filing strategy. Beyond Elevation's 4-Market Filing Framework maps every filing to one of four value drivers: revenue markets, competitor markets, manufacturing markets, and licensing markets. A patent that covers all four costs the same as one that covers only the first.

## What Is the 4-Market Filing Framework?

The Hayat Amin 4-Market Filing Framework is the diagnostic Beyond Elevation runs on every international filing decision. It replaces the default approach — file wherever we have an office — with a value-driven filter that justifies every dollar of prosecution cost.

The four markets are:

**Revenue markets.** Countries where you sell directly or through distributors. A patent here deters copycats and supports pricing power. If you sell $5M in the UK, a UK patent is a cost-of-doing-business filing.

**Competitor markets.** Countries where your direct competitors are headquartered or hold their own patents. Filing here creates a defensive position and cross-licensing leverage. If your main rival is in Germany, a European Patent Office filing is not optional.

**Manufacturing markets.** Countries where your product or its key components are made. A patent in the manufacturing jurisdiction lets you stop copies at the source instead of chasing them across borders. For hardware companies, China is almost always in this category.

**Licensing markets.** Countries where potential licensees operate and generate revenue. If your licensing targets are Japanese electronics companies, a Japanese filing makes the license enforceable. Without it, you are asking them to pay voluntarily.

Most founders file only in revenue markets. That covers one of four value drivers and leaves three unprotected.

## What Are the 3 Mistakes That Waste Your International Patent Budget?

Three mistakes account for the majority of wasted international filing spend. Every one is avoidable with basic planning.

**Filing everywhere instead of strategically.** A founder who files in 15 countries because "we might sell there" will spend $150,000 in prosecution costs before generating a single dollar of IP revenue. Hayat Amin showed one client that 92% of their international filing budget was spent in countries that generated zero revenue, zero licensing interest, and zero competitive threat. They cut to five countries and redirected $80,000 into prosecution quality — broader claims in fewer jurisdictions.

**Missing the 12-month priority deadline.** This is irreversible. Once the priority year lapses, your invention is published prior art worldwide. You cannot file in any new country without the priority claim. According to WIPO data, over 20% of startup founders who intend to file internationally miss this deadline because their attorney did not flag it early enough.

**Choosing countries based on headquarters instead of market opportunity.** A UK-incorporated founder files in the UK by default. But if their largest customer base is in the US and their manufacturing is in Taiwan, the UK patent is the least valuable of the three filings they need. File where the money is, not where the company is registered.

## FAQ

### Can you get a single patent that covers every country?

No. There is no global patent. The PCT lets you file one application that preserves the right to enter individual countries, but each country examines and grants its own patent independently. The closest shortcut is the European Patent Office, which grants one patent that can be validated across up to 38 European states.

### How long does the PCT process take from start to finish?

The PCT international phase lasts 30 months from your priority date. National phase examination typically takes another 12 to 36 months depending on the country and technology. Total time from first filing to granted patents in multiple jurisdictions: 3 to 5 years. Budget accordingly.

### Is the PCT worth it for a startup with limited funds?

Yes. The PCT filing costs $4,000 to $6,000 and buys 30 months to decide where to spend $30,000 or more on national entries. It is the cheapest way to keep your international options open while you raise capital, validate the market, or identify licensing targets. Letting the 12-month priority window close costs far more than the filing ever will.

### Should I file international patents before or after fundraising?

Before — or at minimum, file the PCT before the priority deadline expires. According to the EUIPO's 2024 study, companies with international patent coverage raise 2.4x more capital than those with domestic-only filings. Investors price international defensibility. A PCT filing on your cap table signals that the IP strategy extends beyond one jurisdiction.

### Do I need a patent attorney in every country I file?

Most jurisdictions require a local patent agent or attorney to prosecute your application. Your primary attorney can coordinate these relationships. Budget $2,000 to $5,000 per country for local counsel fees on top of the office fees and translation costs.
