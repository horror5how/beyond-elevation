# Non-Competes Are Dying Across 12 States. Here Is the IP Protection Stack That Replaces Them.

Non-compete agreements are unenforceable or banned in a growing number of US states and UK jurisdictions. The companies that relied on them as their primary IP defence now have no defence at all. The replacement is a five-layer IP protection stack rather than another legal clause. It works whether non-competes are legal in your jurisdiction or not, and it's stronger than the clause it replaces.

According to the US Federal Trade Commission, non-compete clauses affect approximately 30 million American workers, roughly one in five employees bound by a single legal tool. Hayat Amin argues that this was never real IP protection: "A non-compete stops an employee from working for a competitor for 12 months. It does nothing to stop them carrying your trade secrets in their head. The moment the clause expires or becomes unenforceable, every piece of unprotected IP walks out the door."

California banned non-competes decades ago, and it's still home to the most valuable IP portfolios on the planet. The companies with the strongest IP never needed non-competes in the first place.

## Why did companies rely on non-competes for IP protection?

Companies relied on non-competes because they were the cheapest, most convenient substitute for real IP strategy. A non-compete clause costs a few hundred dollars in legal drafting and gets tucked into an employment agreement nobody reads twice. A proper trade secret programme costs tens of thousands to build, requires operational discipline to maintain, and forces uncomfortable conversations about who owns what.

Most startups chose the cheap path. They signed every employee to a non-compete, assumed the IP was locked down, and never built the systems that protect competitive knowledge. The assumption worked when non-competes were enforceable everywhere. That assumption no longer holds.

The core problem: non-competes were always a time-delay mechanism, not a protection mechanism. They delayed a departing employee from competing, typically for 12 to 24 months. They did not prevent the employee from remembering your proprietary processes, your pricing strategy, your customer list, or the engineering decisions that make your product work. Once the non-compete window closed, everything that employee knew was fair game.

Now that window is closing permanently. California, Colorado, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oklahoma, Washington DC, and several other states have banned or severely restricted non-competes. The UK has proposed limiting them to three months. The trend is global and irreversible.

## What does the post-non-compete IP protection stack look like?

The replacement for non-competes is a structured five-layer IP defence that protects your competitive knowledge at every stage of the employee lifecycle, from hiring through departure. Hayat Amin calls this the Five-Layer IP Shield, and [Beyond Elevation](https://beyondelevation.com) deploys it in the first 90 days of every fractional Chief IP Officer engagement.

**Layer 1: IP assignment agreements that hold up.** Most startup IP assignment clauses are boilerplate copied from an incorporation template. They cover "work product" in vague terms and rarely address the specific types of IP your company creates. A proper IP assignment agreement identifies the categories of work that belong to the company, algorithms, training data, customer insights, process innovations, and includes present-tense assignment language ("hereby assigns") rather than a promise to assign in the future. The [IP assignment gap](/insights/employee-ip-assignment-gap/) is the single most common vulnerability found in portfolio audits.

**Layer 2: Trade secret identification and classification.** You cannot protect what you have not identified. A trade secret program starts with a formal audit of every piece of competitive knowledge in the company, from pricing models to engineering playbooks to customer data, and classifies each by sensitivity and business impact. Each classified secret gets documented access controls: who can see it, under what conditions, and what trail they leave. Companies that skip this step discover during litigation that they cannot prove a trade secret existed, let alone that it was stolen.

**Layer 3: Strategic patent filings on competitive differentiators.** Patents do what non-competes never could: they create a legal barrier that survives employee departure, non-compete expiration, and competitor reverse engineering. The key is filing on the innovations that create the most competitive distance, the features that would take a well-funded competitor 18 to 24 months to replicate. Hayat Amin's approach is to [triage every patentable idea](/insights/patent-triage-system-filing-priority/) against a commercial impact score before spending a dollar on filing. Most companies file too many low-value patents and miss the three or four that matter most.

**Layer 4: Invention disclosure workflows.** Engineers create patentable ideas every week without realising it. Without a formal [invention disclosure program](/insights/invention-disclosure-program-ip-capture/), those ideas live in Slack threads and Notion docs, unprotected and unrecorded. A working disclosure workflow captures potential IP at the point of creation, routes it to the right decision-maker, and documents the innovation timeline that patent applications require.

**Layer 5: Exit IP protocols.** The moment an employee resigns, the IP protection clock starts. A proper exit protocol includes a trade secret reminder interview, what confidential information did you have access to?, plus a device and account audit, a signed acknowledgement of ongoing confidentiality obligations, and documented handoff of any IP work in progress. This is not a legal formality. It is the evidence you need if that employee shows up at a competitor three weeks later building suspiciously similar technology.

Companies that do nothing face a compounding risk. Every departing employee carries unprotected IP. Every new hire from a competitor brings potential contamination claims. Every jurisdiction that bans non-competes removes another layer of the only protection these companies ever had.

## FAQ

### Which US states ban or restrict non-compete agreements?

California, Colorado, Minnesota, North Dakota, Oklahoma, and Washington DC have outright bans or near-total restrictions on non-compete agreements. Several other states, including Illinois, Oregon, Washington, and Virginia, impose salary thresholds, time limits, or other significant restrictions that make most non-competes unenforceable for typical employees. The number of restrictive states has roughly doubled since 2020.

### Can non-solicitation agreements replace non-competes for IP protection?

Non-solicitation agreements protect customer relationships, not intellectual property. They prevent a departing employee from poaching clients or colleagues, but they do nothing to stop that employee from using your proprietary processes, algorithms, or trade secrets at a competitor. Non-solicitation is one piece of an employment agreement, not an IP strategy.

### Do non-compete bans apply to C-suite executives and founders?

It depends on the jurisdiction. California's ban applies to everyone regardless of seniority or compensation. Most state-level restrictions apply salary floors, employees earning above a set amount may still be subject to non-competes. The safe assumption for IP protection planning: build your stack as if non-competes will not hold for anyone, because the legal trend is moving in that direction at every seniority level.
