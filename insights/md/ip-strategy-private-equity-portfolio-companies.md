# IP Strategy for Private Equity: The Value Creation Lever Most Firms Never Pull

Most private equity firms run financial due diligence for six months and IP due diligence for two days. That gap is worth millions at exit — and almost every fund leaves it on the table.

According to Ocean Tomo (2024), intangible assets now represent 90 percent of S&P 500 market value. Hayat Amin argues that PE firms buying technology companies without a structured IP strategy for their portfolio are acquiring businesses at valuations set by the seller's narrative — not the buyer's leverage. The IP layer is the single most underworked value creation lever in private equity, and the firms that activate it exit at measurably higher multiples than those that do not.

IP strategy for private equity is not an add-on to your value creation plan. It is the difference between a portfolio company that sells for revenue multiples and one that sells for revenue-plus-defensibility multiples.

## What Is IP Strategy for Private Equity Portfolio Companies?

IP strategy for private equity portfolio companies is a structured programme that identifies, protects, and monetises intellectual property across a fund's holdings — turning undocumented engineering work and dormant data assets into defensible, licensable, and valuation-driving assets before exit. It runs alongside the standard PE value creation playbook, not in place of it.

Most PE value creation plans focus on three levers: revenue growth, margin improvement, and operational efficiency. What they miss is a fourth lever that compounds the other three: intellectual property. A portfolio company with protected IP is harder to compete with, easier to license from, and more expensive to acquire — which is exactly the position you want when you are selling.

Hayat Amin's PE IP Value Creation Playbook breaks this into three phases. Phase one is the IP audit — a 30-day diagnostic that maps every protectable innovation, trade secret, and data asset in the portfolio company. Phase two is the protection sprint — filing provisional patents on the highest-value innovations and formalising trade secret programmes. Phase three is the monetisation layer — identifying licensing opportunities and structuring the IP portfolio for maximum exit premium.

The playbook works because PE holds are finite. Every month of IP value that goes uncaptured is a month closer to exit without the defensibility premium that changes term sheets.

## Why Do Most Private Equity Firms Skip IP Strategy?

Most PE firms skip IP strategy because their operating partners come from finance and operations backgrounds, not IP backgrounds — and their standard due diligence frameworks were designed before intangible assets dominated corporate value. The result is a systematic blind spot that persists across funds of all sizes.

Three specific reasons explain the gap.

First, PE deal teams treat IP as a legal checkbox, not a value creation lever. The IP section of due diligence typically asks whether the company owns its IP and whether there are pending lawsuits. It does not ask whether the company has unfiled patents, unprotected trade secrets, or unlicensed data assets worth millions. The questions are defensive, not offensive.

Second, most portfolio company management teams do not know what IP they have. Engineers build patentable innovations every sprint and document them nowhere. Proprietary data assets sit in databases with no valuation, no licensing structure, and no protection beyond a standard employment agreement. When Hayat Amin ran the IP discovery process on one portfolio company, the team identified 11 patent-eligible innovations in a single session — innovations the CTO had dismissed as routine engineering.

Third, there is no standard PE role responsible for IP value creation. The CFO manages financial value. The COO manages operational value. Nobody manages intellectual property value — which is why the fractional Chief IP Officer role exists.

## What Does the First 100 Days of a PE IP Strategy Look Like?

The first 100 days of a PE IP strategy for a newly acquired portfolio company follow a structured sequence that maps IP assets, files protections, and builds the exit narrative — all before the first board review. Speed matters because every quarter without IP capture is a quarter of unprotected innovation and unrealised value.

**Days 1 to 30: IP Discovery and Audit.** Run structured technical interviews with the engineering, product, and data teams. Catalogue every innovation, trade secret, proprietary dataset, and unique process. Map each asset against competitor activity and the patent landscape. The output is an IP asset register with preliminary valuations using the cost-to-recreate method. Hayat Amin says this step alone changes the management team's understanding of what they own: founders consistently underestimate the IP embedded in their own engineering work by a factor of three to five.

**Days 31 to 60: Protection Sprint.** File provisional patent applications on the five to ten highest-value innovations. Formalise the trade secret programme: classify confidential information, implement access controls, and ensure every employee and contractor has signed appropriate IP assignment agreements. This phase costs £15,000 to £40,000 depending on portfolio company complexity — a fraction of the value it protects.

**Days 61 to 100: Monetisation and Exit Positioning.** Identify non-competing verticals where the portfolio company's IP could generate licensing revenue. Build the IP section of the exit narrative: documented patents, structured trade secrets, clean data asset provenance, and a licensing pipeline that shows revenue optionality. This is the material that moves the exit multiple from revenue-based to revenue-plus-defensibility-based.

## What IP Value Creation Levers Actually Move the Exit Multiple?

Four specific IP levers create measurable enterprise value in a PE portfolio company, and each one is quantifiable in the exit model. Companies with patents are 10.2 times more likely to secure early-stage funding — and the same defensibility premium applies at exit, where acquirers pay 30 to 60 percent more for businesses with structured IP portfolios.

**Patent portfolio.** Granted patents on core technology create legal barriers that acquirers value because they reduce competitive risk post-acquisition. Five to ten well-targeted patents covering the portfolio company's key differentiators can add 15 to 30 percent to the exit multiple versus a comparable unpatented business.

**Trade secret programme.** A formalised trade secret programme — documented, classified, access-controlled — signals operational maturity and reduces key-person risk. Acquirers discount companies where proprietary knowledge lives in the heads of three engineers who could leave. A structured programme makes the knowledge transferable and defensible.

**Data asset valuation.** Portfolio companies sitting on proprietary datasets — customer behaviour data, operational telemetry, domain-specific training data — often have assets worth millions that appear nowhere in their financials. Documenting and valuing these assets adds a line item to the IP register that acquirers price separately from the core business. For a primer on this process, see Beyond Elevation's guide to [valuing your data](/insights/value-your-data/).

**Licensing revenue.** Active licensing income from the IP portfolio demonstrates market validation and creates a recurring revenue stream that acquirers value at high multiples. Even a modest licensing programme generating £200,000 to £500,000 per year adds significant enterprise value because it proves the IP has commercial relevance beyond the company's own products.

The PE firms that activate all four levers do not just sell portfolio companies. They sell portfolio companies that acquirers compete to buy — and competitive bidding is where the real exit premium lives.

## FAQ

### How much does an IP strategy cost for a PE portfolio company?

A complete IP strategy engagement — from audit through filing and monetisation positioning — typically costs £60,000 to £150,000 over the first year, depending on the complexity of the portfolio company's technology stack. This includes the IP audit, provisional patent filings, trade secret programme implementation, and exit narrative development. The return is measurable in the exit multiple: a well-structured IP portfolio routinely adds 20 to 40 percent to enterprise value.

### When should a PE firm start IP strategy after acquiring a company?

Start within the first 30 days of closing. The IP discovery audit should run in parallel with the standard 100-day plan. Every quarter of delay is a quarter where engineers create unprotected innovations, competitors file on adjacent technology, and the exit narrative lacks the defensibility premium that changes term sheets. Hayat Amin argues that IP strategy should be part of pre-acquisition due diligence — but if it was not, the first month post-close is the second-best time.

### Can a fractional CIPO work across multiple portfolio companies in the same fund?

Yes. A fractional Chief IP Officer is designed to serve multiple companies simultaneously. The IP audit and protection sprint for each portfolio company requires concentrated effort for 60 to 90 days, then transitions to ongoing portfolio management at two to three days per month. One fractional CIPO can effectively serve three to five portfolio companies, with engagement intensity scaling around key milestones like product launches, fundraising rounds, and exit preparation.

### What IP issues most commonly reduce exit value in PE deals?

The three most common IP issues that reduce exit value are: unclear ownership of founder-created code or pre-incorporation IP, unprotected trade secrets that live only in employee knowledge, and unfiled patents on core technology that competitors could patent first. Each of these is preventable with a structured IP strategy implemented during the hold period — and each one, left unaddressed, gives the acquirer leverage to discount the purchase price by 15 to 30 percent.
