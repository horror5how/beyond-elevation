# 73% of Funded Startups Pivot. Their IP Strategy Breaks the Same Day.

Seventy-three percent of funded startups pivot at least once before finding product-market fit, according to a 2025 Startup Genome study. The day they pivot, every patent claim they filed is still pointed at the old product, the old market, the old competitive set. According to Hayat Amin, the IP strategist who has restructured portfolios through more than a dozen pivots, the patent portfolio is the last thing founders update and the first thing investors check. The result is predictable: a startup walks into a Series A pitch with patents that protect a product it no longer sells.

Beyond Elevation sees this pattern repeat every quarter. A deep-tech startup pivots from hardware to SaaS, or from B2C to B2B, or from one vertical to another. The engineering team rebuilds the product in weeks. The IP portfolio sits unchanged for months. By the time the next funding round arrives, the due diligence reveals a patent portfolio that is a museum exhibit — technically impressive, commercially irrelevant.

## Why Does a Startup Pivot Break Your IP Strategy?

A startup pivot breaks your IP strategy because patent claims are written against specific technical implementations, specific use cases, and specific markets. When the product changes, the claims do not change with it. A patent that covered a novel sensor-fusion algorithm for autonomous drones does not automatically cover the same algorithm repurposed for warehouse robotics. The claims may be broad enough to stretch — or they may not. Without a structured triage, founders are guessing.

The damage compounds in three ways. First, maintenance fees keep draining cash on patents that no longer protect the business. The average patent portfolio costs $15,000 to $40,000 per year in maintenance and prosecution fees across a handful of active filings. For a post-pivot startup burning cash, that spend is indefensible unless the patents map to the new direction. Second, investors see the mismatch in due diligence and discount the IP premium accordingly. Hayat Amin argues that a misaligned patent portfolio is worse than no portfolio at all — it signals that the founder does not understand what they own. Third, the new product may be infringing on competitors' patents in the new market, and no one has checked because no one ran a freedom-to-operate analysis after the pivot.

## What Is the Hayat Amin IP Pivot Triage?

The IP Pivot Triage is a five-step framework Beyond Elevation runs within 30 days of any material change in product direction. It sorts every IP asset in the portfolio into one of four buckets: keep, refile, license out, or abandon. The goal is not to save every patent. The goal is to make the portfolio match the business the company is actually building.

The framework works because it treats the pivot as an IP event, not just a product event. Most startup advisors treat the pivot as a strategy decision, a fundraising story, and a product rebuild. Hayat Amin says the IP triage is the step they all skip — and the one that determines whether the next raise prices in an IP premium or an IP discount.

### Step 1: Map Every Claim to the New Product

Take every granted patent and pending application. Read the claims — not the abstract, not the specification, the actual claims. For each claim, ask one question: does this claim cover something the new product does, or something the old product did? If the answer is old product only, the claim is a candidate for licensing or abandonment. If the answer is both, the claim survives. If the new product does something novel that no existing claim covers, that is a filing gap.

### Step 2: Run a Freedom-to-Operate Scan on the New Market

The new market has incumbents with their own patent portfolios. A B2C-to-B2B pivot, a vertical shift, or a technology repurposing can land the startup directly in a competitor's claim scope. A freedom-to-operate analysis within 30 days of the pivot identifies blocking patents before the company ships the new product into the new market. Skipping this step is how post-pivot startups get hit with cease-and-desist letters six months after launch.

### Step 3: Identify Licensing Revenue From Orphaned Patents

Patents that no longer protect the new business may still have value in the old market. If a competitor is still operating in the space the startup left, those orphaned patents become licensable assets. Hayat Amin reminds founders that an orphaned patent is not a sunk cost — it is a revenue opportunity. Beyond Elevation has structured licensing deals on orphaned post-pivot patents that generated enough revenue to fund the next two quarters of R&D in the new direction. The key is acting fast: the longer a patent sits idle, the harder it becomes to demonstrate its commercial relevance to a potential licensee.

### Step 4: File Provisional Applications on the New Innovation

The pivot almost always involves new technical innovation — a new architecture, a new data pipeline, a new integration method, a new user experience. File provisional patent applications on the novel elements within 60 days of the pivot. Provisional applications cost $1,500 to $3,000 each and establish a priority date. That priority date is what matters: if a competitor files on a similar innovation in the new market six months later, the startup that filed first wins. Hayat Amin's rule is simple — file the provisional before you announce the pivot publicly, because a public announcement can trigger a one-year clock for patent filing in the US and can destroy patentability entirely in most other jurisdictions.

### Step 5: Kill the Deadweight

Abandon patents that fail three tests: they do not cover the new product, they are not licensable in the old market, and they do not create a defensive position against potential competitors. Abandonment frees maintenance budget, simplifies the portfolio for due diligence, and signals to investors that someone is actively managing the IP — not just accumulating filings and hoping for the best. A clean, tight portfolio of eight relevant patents is worth more in a fundraise than a sprawling portfolio of twenty that includes twelve relics from a product the company no longer builds.

## How Does a Pivot Create an IP Opportunity?

A pivot creates an IP opportunity because the new market is often less crowded with patent filings than the old one. When a startup moves from a heavily patented sector — like autonomous vehicles or fintech — into an adjacent vertical with fewer incumbents, the white space for new patent claims is wider. The 90-day window after a pivot is the single best filing opportunity most founders waste. Competitors in the new market have not seen you coming. They have not filed on the intersection of your old technology and their new use case. The founder who files first on that intersection owns it for 20 years.

This is where the IP strategy shifts from defensive to offensive. Instead of protecting what you built, you are staking out territory in the new market before anyone else maps it. Beyond Elevation structures these filings as a cluster — three to five patents covering different aspects of the same technical approach applied to the new market — because a cluster is exponentially harder to design around than a single filing.

## What Happens If You Skip the IP Triage After a Pivot?

If you skip the IP triage after a pivot, three things happen in sequence. First, you spend $15,000 to $40,000 per year maintaining patents that protect a product you no longer sell. Second, your next investor or acquirer runs their own IP review and discovers the mismatch — the typical valuation discount for a misaligned IP portfolio is 15 to 25 percent, according to a 2026 FE International analysis of 140 tech M&A transactions. Third, a competitor in your new market files patents on the same technical approach you are using, and you have no prior art and no priority date to challenge them. All three are preventable with a 30-day triage that costs a fraction of a single patent prosecution.

## FAQ

### How soon after a pivot should I review my IP strategy?

Within 30 days. The IP Pivot Triage is designed to run in this window because provisional filing deadlines, maintenance fee decisions, and freedom-to-operate risks all compound with delay. The longer you wait, the more you spend on irrelevant patents and the more filing opportunities you lose in the new market.

### Can I license patents that no longer fit my business after a pivot?

Yes. Patents that covered your old product may still be valuable to competitors operating in the market you left. Beyond Elevation has structured licensing deals on orphaned post-pivot patents that generated meaningful revenue. The key is identifying licensees quickly — within three to six months of the pivot — before the technology becomes outdated.

### Does a pivot affect my freedom to operate in the new market?

Almost always. The new market has its own patent landscape, and the technology you are bringing may overlap with existing claims held by incumbents. A freedom-to-operate analysis after a pivot is essential to avoid infringement risk before you ship the new product.

### Should I abandon patents that do not fit the new direction?

Abandon patents that fail all three tests: they do not protect the new product, they are not licensable, and they do not create a useful defensive position. Keeping dead patents wastes maintenance budget and clutters due diligence. A focused portfolio of relevant patents commands a higher valuation multiple than a bloated portfolio of irrelevant ones.
