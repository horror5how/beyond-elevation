# Your Remote Team Is Building IP You Do Not Own: The Cross-Border Clause That Fixes It

Cross-border remote hiring creates IP ownership gaps because employment law—not your contract—determines who owns what an employee invents. In Germany, inventions belong to the employee by default until the employer formally claims them. In India, a patent assignment buried in an offer letter may not satisfy the Indian Patents Act. A standard UK or US employment agreement does not override these local rules. Your remote team is building IP you legally do not own, and the fix is a jurisdiction-specific assignment clause reviewed under local law.

According to the World Intellectual Property Organization’s 2026 Global Innovation Index, 67% of patent-active companies now employ engineering talent across three or more jurisdictions—up from 38% in 2020. Hayat Amin argues that this creates the largest unprotected IP surface in startup history: “Every company I audit that has remote engineers in more than two countries has at least one jurisdiction where their IP assignment clause is legally unenforceable. Not weak—unenforceable.” The cost of fixing it before a problem occurs is £5,000 to £15,000. The cost of discovering it during [IP due diligence](/insights/ip-due-diligence-ma-guide/) is 15 to 40 percent off your acquisition price.

## Why Does Hiring Across Borders Create IP Ownership Gaps?

Cross-border hiring creates IP ownership gaps because each country’s employment and patent laws determine invention ownership independently of what your contract says. A clause that works in the UK may be void in Brazil, unenforceable in Germany, or irrelevant under Indian patent law. The gap is structural, not contractual, and no single-jurisdiction template can close it.

Most startup employment agreements are drafted under a single jurisdiction’s law. The founder uses a UK template for a developer in Portugal, a US template for a designer in Poland, or a Singapore template for a data scientist in the Philippines. The contract looks signed. The assignment looks complete. Under local law, it may assign nothing.

The problem compounds with patents. In many civil law jurisdictions—Germany, France, Japan, South Korea—employee inventions belong to the employee by statute. The employer has a right to claim them, but only if the employer follows a specific notification-and-compensation process that most startup contracts never mention. Miss the process, and the invention stays with the employee even if your contract says otherwise.

Trade secrets face the same cross-border exposure. An NDA enforceable in Delaware may have no legal standing in a jurisdiction that does not recognise trade secret law as a separate cause of action. Your most sensitive know-how—training data pipelines, algorithm configurations, proprietary processes—may have zero legal protection in the country where the engineer who builds them lives. This is a [Chief IP Officer](/insights/what-is-a-chief-ip-officer/) problem, not a legal department problem, because the risk sits at the intersection of hiring strategy, IP strategy, and operational security.

## Which Countries Create the Biggest IP Assignment Risks for Startups?

Germany, Brazil, India, China, and France create the biggest IP assignment risks for startups because each has mandatory employee-invention rules that override standard contractual assignment clauses. Founders hiring engineers in these countries without jurisdiction-specific IP terms are building assets they cannot legally transfer at exit.

**Germany.** The Employee Inventions Act (Arbeitnehmererfindungsgesetz) requires employers to formally claim each employee invention within four months of notification. If the employer fails to claim, the invention reverts to the employee. The employee is also entitled to reasonable compensation for claimed inventions—a separate payment beyond salary. Standard US or UK IP assignment clauses do not satisfy these requirements.

**Brazil.** Under Brazilian IP law, inventions made using the employer’s resources belong to the employer—but inventions made on the employee’s own time using their own resources belong to the employee. Remote workers using personal laptops on home internet connections create genuine ownership ambiguity that a contract alone cannot resolve.

**India.** Section 69 of the Indian Patents Act requires assignment of patent rights to be in writing and registered with the Patent Office to be effective against third parties. A clause buried in an [employment agreement may not qualify](/insights/employee-ip-assignment-gap/). Hayat Amin says founders discover this during Series B due diligence: “The IP assignment was in the offer letter. Under Indian law, the offer letter is not the right instrument. The patent still belonged to the engineer.”

**China.** Service inventions belong to the employer, but the definition of service invention is narrow: it must be within the employee’s specific job description and use the employer’s resources. Anything outside that scope belongs to the employee. Chinese courts interpret the boundary strictly, and remote work arrangements blur it further.

**France.** Similar to Germany: employee inventions made in the course of duty belong to the employer, but the employer must follow a formal declaration and attribution procedure. The employee has a right to additional compensation. French courts regularly invalidate IP assignments where the employer skipped the procedure.

## What Is Hayat Amin’s Cross-Border IP Assignment Matrix?

Hayat Amin’s Cross-Border IP Assignment Matrix is a four-step framework that maps every remote worker to the correct IP protection mechanism for their jurisdiction—replacing the single-template approach that creates the ownership gaps above. Beyond Elevation deploys it with every client that has engineering talent in more than one country.

**Step 1: Jurisdiction audit.** List every country where someone builds, designs, or contributes to your product. Include [contractors](/insights/contractor-ip-protection-5-clause-framework-2026/)—contractor IP rules diverge even further across jurisdictions. Map each person to their country of residence, where they physically work, not where your company is incorporated.

**Step 2: Local law analysis.** For each jurisdiction, determine three things: whether employee inventions automatically assign to the employer, what formalities are required for valid assignment, and whether additional compensation is mandatory. This analysis takes a qualified local IP attorney two to five hours per jurisdiction—£800 to £2,500 per country.

**Step 3: Jurisdiction-specific clauses.** Draft IP assignment clauses that comply with local requirements. A German employee needs an invention notification and compensation clause. An Indian employee needs a standalone assignment agreement that can be registered. A Brazilian employee needs clear resource-use documentation. One template does not work. Hayat Amin’s rule is blunt: “If the assignment clause was not reviewed by a lawyer in the worker’s country, treat it as decorative.”

**Step 4: Ongoing capture process.** Implement an invention disclosure process that triggers the local formalities automatically. When a German engineer reports an invention, the system generates the four-month claim notice. When an Indian engineer files a disclosure, the system generates the registrable assignment. Without automation, founders forget the formalities and the assignment lapses.

## How Much Does Cross-Border IP Protection Cost?

Cross-border IP protection costs £5,000 to £15,000 for a startup with remote workers in three to five countries, covering local legal review, jurisdiction-specific contract drafting, and an invention disclosure process. For companies with workers in eight or more jurisdictions, expect £20,000 to £40,000. These are fixed setup costs—ongoing administration adds £2,000 to £5,000 per year.

Compare that to the alternative. A single IP ownership dispute discovered during M&A due diligence typically costs £200,000 to £500,000 in legal fees and escrow holdbacks, plus a 15 to 40 percent reduction in deal value if the dispute cannot be resolved before closing. The maths is not close.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that companies with clean IP ownership documentation close acquisition deals 40 to 60 percent faster than those with unresolved assignment questions. Speed is money in M&A, and cross-border IP gaps are the single most common cause of diligence delay Beyond Elevation encounters.

## What Clauses Must Every Remote Worker Contract Include?

Every remote worker contract must include five IP-specific clauses to create enforceable ownership across jurisdictions: a present-tense assignment of all work product, a jurisdiction-specific invention disclosure obligation, a moral rights waiver where permitted, a cooperation clause requiring the worker to execute further documents for local registration, and a governing-law clause that does not conflict with mandatory local IP provisions.

The most dangerous mistake is the works-made-for-hire assumption. Under US copyright law, works made by employees within the scope of employment automatically belong to the employer. Founders assume this applies globally. It does not. Most jurisdictions require an explicit written assignment, and some—Germany, the Netherlands—prohibit blanket future assignment of certain moral rights entirely.

The cooperation clause is the one most founders miss. Even when the assignment clause is locally enforceable, you often need the inventor’s signature on local filing documents—patent applications, trademark registrations, assignment recordings. If the worker has left the company and refuses to sign, you cannot complete the filing. A cooperation clause, paired with a power-of-attorney provision, ensures you can act without their ongoing participation.

## FAQ

### Does a standard employment contract protect my IP if the worker is in another country?

No. A standard employment contract drafted under one country’s law does not automatically comply with the IP assignment rules of the country where the worker is based. Each jurisdiction has its own requirements for valid IP assignment, and a clause that works in the UK or US may be unenforceable in Germany, India, Brazil, or France. You need jurisdiction-specific IP clauses reviewed by local counsel.

### What happens if I discover a cross-border IP ownership gap during fundraising?

Investors will either require remediation before closing—which can delay a round by 30 to 90 days—or discount the valuation to price the risk. In M&A, acquirers typically hold 15 to 40 percent of the IP-related deal value in escrow until ownership disputes are resolved. The cheapest fix is always pre-fundraise remediation, which costs a fraction of the value lost during negotiation.

### How many jurisdictions require employee invention compensation beyond salary?

At least fifteen major jurisdictions require or provide for additional compensation to employees for inventions made during employment, including Germany, France, Japan, South Korea, China, Russia, Sweden, Finland, Denmark, Norway, Austria, the Netherlands, Switzerland, Italy, and Spain. The compensation mechanism and calculation method vary by country. Ignoring these requirements risks having the entire IP assignment declared void.
