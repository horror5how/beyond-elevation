# Startup Founder Salary: The Number by Stage and the Mistake 62 Percent of Founders Make

A fractional CFO's first question in every founder engagement is about founder salary, not revenue, margins or burn rate. Hayat Amin, who has structured founder compensation across three prior exits and dozens of fractional CFO engagements at Beyond Elevation, says the number a founder pays themselves is the single fastest signal of financial maturity an investor reads, and 62% of seed-stage founders never formally agree that number with their board.

According to a 2026 Kruze Consulting analysis of 450 venture-backed startups, the median seed-stage founder salary sits at $82,000 (roughly £65,000), yet the range spans from zero to $225,000. That spread reflects confusion, not ambition. It costs companies twice: underpay the founder and they burn out before Series A. Overpay and the runway shrinks from 24 months to 16.

## What should a startup founder pay themselves at each stage?

A startup founder's salary should be set by funding stage, cash position and board composition, not by market benchmarks or personal need. Pre-seed founders typically draw $0 to 40,000, seed-funded founders $60,000 to 100,000, Series A founders $120,000 to 175,000, and Series B and later founders 70 to 80% of what an equivalent permanent executive would earn on the open market. The principle is the same at every stage: the salary must remove personal financial stress without signalling to investors that the founder optimises for cash compensation over equity upside.

The numbers follow a runway-preservation formula. At seed stage, founder salary typically represents 8 to 12% of monthly burn. Anything above 15% and the board starts asking questions. Anything below 5% and the founder is subsidising the company with personal risk, a hidden liability no balance sheet shows.

Hayat Amin's rule at Beyond Elevation is direct: "The founder salary is not a reward. It is a tool. Set it wrong and you either lose the founder or lose the runway. Both kill the company."

## Why do most founders get their startup founder salary wrong?

Most founders default to one of two extremes, and both signal the same thing to a sophisticated board: this founder doesn't understand capital allocation. The two failure modes are predictable. The martyr founder pays themselves nothing or near-nothing, burns personal savings for 18 months, and quits three months before the fundraise closes. The market-rate founder pulls $180,000 from a $2M raise and compresses 24 months of runway into 15.

The root cause is identical in both cases: founders treat salary as a personal decision when it's a capital allocation decision. A fractional CFO reframes it as a line item in the financial model, stress-tested against three scenarios: base case, downside, and delayed-fundraise.

The delayed-fundraise scenario is the one that exposes bad founder salary decisions. If the next round takes six months longer than planned, which happens in roughly 40% of raises according to Carta's 2025 fundraising data, does the current salary hold without forcing an emergency bridge? If the answer is no, the salary is too high. If the answer is "yes, but the founder can't cover rent," the salary is too low.

## What framework does a fractional CFO use for founder compensation?

A fractional CFO ties founder salary to cash position and months of runway, not revenue or market benchmarks. Beyond Elevation runs a four-variable test before recommending any founder salary: months of remaining runway, the founder's personal burn rate, board composition, and the timeline to the next fundraise.

Hayat Amin built the **Founder Compensation Runway Test** after watching three portfolio companies lose founders to burnout while sitting on more than 20 months of runway. All three were paying founders under $40,000 in a major metro market.

The test has four steps:

**Step 1:** Calculate monthly cash burn excluding founder salary.

**Step 2:** Add the proposed founder salary. Does total burn still leave 18 or more months of runway?

**Step 3:** Run the delayed-fundraise scenario. Add six months to the next raise timeline. Does the salary still hold?

**Step 4:** Check the number against the board's expectations. If the board hasn't discussed it, that's the first problem to fix.

If the salary passes all four steps, it's defensible. If it fails Step 2, it's too high. If the founder can't cover personal expenses at the Step 2 number, the raise was too small, not the salary too low.

## How does founder salary change after a funding round?

Founder salary resets at every funding round because the capital base, board composition and investor expectations change at once. After a Series A close, most term sheets include a compensation clause or a board-approved salary range, and the founder who hasn't proposed their own number before that clause arrives has already lost the negotiation.

The pattern is predictable. Pre-seed to seed: the founder proposes, angel investors approve with minimal scrutiny. Seed to Series A: the lead investor's term sheet includes a compensation policy, usually capping founder salary at $130,000, $175,000. Series A to Series B: the board sets a formal compensation committee, and founder salary is benchmarked against the company's own executive band.

Hayat Amin tells founders to agree their salary with the board 30 days before the term sheet arrives, not after. "Every founder who waits for the investor to set the number ends up with a number the investor chose. That is not negotiation. That is delegation."

## What is the biggest founder compensation mistake at board level?

The biggest mistake is not discussing founder compensation at all. In a 2025 SeedLegals analysis of 3,200 UK startups, 62% of seed-stage founders had no formal board resolution on founder salary. The salary was inherited from the pre-funding period, often zero or a nominal amount that made sense when the company had three months of personal savings, not $1.5M in the bank.

The absence of a formal discussion creates two risks. First, the founder sets their own salary by default, which looks self-serving if too high or irrational if too low once the Series A investor runs due diligence on historical payroll. Second, the company has no documented rationale for the number, so the tax authority sees a potential benefit-in-kind or below-market-rate flag.

Beyond Elevation's fractional CFOs draft a board resolution for founder compensation in every new engagement. It takes 20 minutes, costs nothing, and removes a due diligence question that derails 1 in 8 fundraises.

## Should founders take a salary cut to extend runway?

A founder salary cut should be the last lever pulled, not the first, because it trades visible cash savings for invisible productivity loss. Hayat Amin's order is fixed: cut discretionary spend first, renegotiate vendor contracts second, reduce headcount third, cut founder salary fourth. A founder earning $100,000 who cuts to $50,000 saves $50,000 a year, roughly one month of runway for a 15-person company. The same founder working at 60% capacity because they can't cover their mortgage costs the company far more in missed milestones and slower decisions.

The exception is a bridge scenario, where three to six months of reduced founder salary ties to a specific funding milestone. That's a short-term capital preservation agreement with a defined end date, not a salary cut, and it should be documented as a board resolution with a reversion clause.

## FAQ

### How much should a pre-seed founder pay themselves?

Pre-seed founders typically draw $0 to $40,000 a year. The number depends on personal burn rate and whether the company has external funding or is bootstrapped. If the company has raised a pre-seed round, paying nothing signals poor capital allocation, not frugality.

### Can a founder increase their salary after raising a round?

Yes, and they should. Every funding round changes the capital base and the demands on the founder's time. The salary should reset to reflect the new runway, board composition, and next-round timeline. Propose the number to the board within 30 days of close.

### What percentage of burn should founder salary represent?

At seed stage, founder salary typically runs 8 to 12% of monthly burn. Above 15% draws board scrutiny. Below 5% creates a hidden burnout risk. The percentage falls naturally as the team grows and total burn increases.

### Should co-founders always earn the same salary?

Not necessarily. Equal salary is the default, but not always right. If one co-founder works full time and the other part time, or one carries far higher personal financial obligations, differentiated salaries are defensible, as long as they're documented in a board resolution and both founders agree.

### Does founder salary affect company valuation?

Indirectly, yes. Excessive founder salary cuts runway, which cuts the time available to hit the milestones that drive valuation. A salary that's too low creates key-person risk: if the founder leaves because of financial stress, the company's value drops immediately. The right salary protects both runway and retention.
