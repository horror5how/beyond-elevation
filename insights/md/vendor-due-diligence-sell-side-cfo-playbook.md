# Vendor Due Diligence: The Sell-Side CFO Playbook That Stops Buyers From Setting the Price

Vendor due diligence is the financial audit a seller runs on itself before going to market, and companies that skip it hand the buyer a discount. According to Deloitte's 2026 M&A Integration Report, sell-side transactions backed by vendor due diligence close 27 percent faster and suffer 40 percent fewer price reductions during negotiation. Hayat Amin argues this is the single most cost-effective move a seller can make: a five-figure investment that protects six or seven figures of deal value.

Most founders hear the phrase for the first time after a buyer flags an issue in their numbers, and by then the negotiation has already tipped the buyer's way. The buyer owns the surprise and the seller is left explaining it. The price comes down, the timeline stretches, and a chunk of enterprise value disappears in a conversation that never needed to happen.

## What is vendor due diligence and why does the seller run it?

Vendor due diligence is a financial, tax, and commercial review that the selling company commissions and controls before inviting buyer scrutiny. The seller hires an independent advisor to audit the same areas a buyer would examine, revenue quality, working capital, normalised earnings, tax exposure, and contingent liabilities, then packages the findings into a report the buyer can rely on.

The logic is straightforward. In a standard sale process, the buyer runs their own due diligence and surfaces every issue they find, and each issue becomes a negotiating lever that pulls the price down. Vendor due diligence reverses that dynamic. The seller finds the issues first, fixes what can be fixed, explains what cannot, and presents the buyer with a clean, pre-verified picture. The buyer still runs confirmatory work, but the surprises are gone, and so are most of the price chips.

Hayat Amin's rule on this is blunt: if the buyer discovers a problem in your numbers before you do, you have already lost money. The only question is how much.

## What financial areas does vendor due diligence cover?

A sell-side vendor due diligence report covers the same terrain a buyer's accountants would examine, but from the seller's perspective and on the seller's timeline. The core areas are revenue quality, earnings normalisation, working capital analysis, tax compliance, and contingent liabilities.

**Revenue quality.** The report breaks revenue into recurring and one-off, contracted and exposed, concentrated and diversified. A buyer wants to see that revenue will survive the ownership change. Any customer concentration above 15 percent of total revenue gets flagged. Any revenue recognition timing that could be questioned gets documented and defended.

**Earnings normalisation.** Every private company runs expenses through the business that a buyer will strip out, owner compensation, related-party transactions, one-off legal costs, above-market rent to a connected landlord. The VDD report normalises EBITDA to show what the business earns on a standalone, arm's-length basis. This is usually the single biggest value lever. A properly normalised EBITDA can shift the implied enterprise value by 15 to 30 percent compared to the raw management accounts.

**Working capital.** Buyers set a target working capital level and adjust the purchase price if actual working capital at completion deviates from it. Hayat Amin reminds sellers that the working capital mechanism is where more post-completion disputes start than any other clause in the SPA. The VDD report establishes a defensible baseline, analyses seasonality, and flags any items the buyer will challenge, slow-moving inventory, overdue receivables, accruals that look light.

**Tax compliance.** Open tax positions, transfer pricing exposure, R&D tax credit sustainability, and historic filing accuracy all get reviewed. A buyer finding an unresolved tax risk will either demand an indemnity or reduce the price. Finding it first and resolving it, or ring-fencing it with a warranty, costs far less than handing the buyer that advantage at the negotiating table.

**Contingent liabilities.** Pending litigation, warranty claims, regulatory investigations, earn-out obligations from prior acquisitions, and any off-balance-sheet commitments get mapped. The goal is a clean schedule of known exposures with quantified ranges, not a surprise the buyer's lawyers find in the data room at two in the morning.

## How does vendor due diligence change the deal?

Vendor due diligence shifts negotiating power from the buyer to the seller in three concrete ways. First, it compresses the deal timeline. The buyer's confirmatory due diligence runs in weeks instead of months because the heavy financial analysis is already done, verified, and packaged. Shorter timelines mean less deal fatigue, fewer excuses to retrade, and lower execution risk.

Second, it reduces price chips. Every issue the buyer discovers independently becomes a reason to adjust the price downward or demand a larger escrow holdback. Hayat Amin's experience across dozens of sell-side engagements shows that undiscovered issues found by the buyer cost the seller two to five times what the same issue costs when the seller discloses it first. The asymmetry exists because a buyer who discovers a problem also discovers that the seller either did not know about it or chose not to disclose it, both of which erode trust and invite aggressive repricing.

Third, it supports competitive tension. In a process with multiple bidders, a VDD report lets every bidder assess the business quickly and on the same information. This keeps the auction competitive. Without it, each bidder runs their own due diligence at their own pace, the process fragments, and the seller loses the pressure of competing timelines.

## When should a seller start vendor due diligence?

The ideal window to begin vendor due diligence is six to nine months before going to market. Starting earlier gives the seller time to fix material issues, cleaning up revenue recognition, resolving open tax positions, renegotiating related-party contracts at arm's length, or documenting key-person dependencies that a buyer will flag.

Starting later still works, but the seller loses the ability to fix problems and is left only disclosing and defending them. Hayat Amin's Seller-Side Financial Shield framework breaks the preparation into three phases: diagnose at month minus nine, remediate at month minus six, and package at month minus three. The diagnose phase identifies every issue a buyer's accountants would find. The remediate phase fixes the fixable items and builds the narrative around the rest. The package phase produces the final VDD report, structures the data room, and prepares management for buyer Q&A sessions.

## What does vendor due diligence cost and what does it save?

Vendor due diligence costs depend on the complexity of the business, the number of jurisdictions, and the scope of the report. For a company in the UK with a single entity and straightforward operations, expect 30,000 to 60,000 pounds for the VDD report from a mid-tier accounting firm. Businesses with multiple entities across borders can run 80,000 to 150,000 pounds. Adding tax and commercial due diligence to the financial scope adds 20 to 40 percent to the total.

The return is disproportionate. On a transaction with an enterprise value of 10 million pounds, a single normalisation adjustment that increases EBITDA by 200,000 pounds at a 6x multiple creates 1.2 million pounds of additional deal value. A working capital baseline that prevents a 150,000-pound completion adjustment pays for the entire VDD engagement three times over. And the timeline compression, closing weeks earlier, reduces advisor fees, management distraction, and the risk of a market shock derailing the deal.

## FAQ

### Who pays for vendor due diligence, the buyer or the seller?

The seller commissions and pays for vendor due diligence. The seller's advisors prepare the report. The buyer may rely on the report for confirmatory purposes but typically still engages their own advisors to review it. The cost sits with the seller because the seller controls the process, the timing, and the narrative.

### Is vendor due diligence the same as an audit?

No. An audit provides an opinion on whether financial statements present a true and fair view under accounting standards. Vendor due diligence does more. It analyses the quality of earnings, normalises EBITDA for non-recurring items, assesses working capital adequacy, reviews tax positions, and identifies risks a buyer would price. An audit is backward-looking compliance. VDD is forward-looking commercial analysis designed for a transaction.

### Can a fractional CFO run vendor due diligence?

Yes. A fractional CFO manages the sell-side financial workstream, commissioning the VDD report, coordinating with the accounting firm, structuring the data room, preparing management for buyer meetings, and negotiating working capital and completion mechanisms. The fractional CFO does not write the VDD report itself, that comes from an independent accounting firm, but the CFO owns the process, the timeline, and the commercial decisions around disclosure and remediation. This is one of the highest-ROI engagements a [fractional CFO focused on exit preparation](/insights/fractional-cfo-exit-preparation/) can deliver.

### What happens if the seller does not run vendor due diligence?

The buyer runs their own due diligence and controls the discovery process. Every issue they find becomes a negotiating lever, so the seller ends up reacting rather than leading. The timeline extends, price chips accumulate, and post-completion disputes increase. For transactions above 5 million pounds in enterprise value, skipping VDD is almost always a false economy. Sellers typically lose that saved money several times over in deal repricing and extended negotiations.

### How long does vendor due diligence take?

The VDD report itself takes four to eight weeks to produce once the accounting firm is engaged and data is flowing. The broader preparation, fixing issues, structuring the data room, preparing management, should start six to nine months before going to market. A [finance function that is already exit-ready](/insights/exit-ready-finance-function/) can compress this timeline. One running on spreadsheets and a bookkeeper working two days a week cannot.
