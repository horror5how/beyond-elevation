# Your Company Created IP All Year. Nobody Checked Whether You Still Own It. The Year-End IP Review Checklist.

## What Is a Year-End IP Review?

A year-end IP review is a structured audit of every intellectual property asset a company created, acquired, licensed, or let lapse during the calendar year. It catches protection gaps, deadline risks, ownership ambiguities, and valuation misalignments before they compound into Q1 problems. Unlike a full IP audit, which maps an entire portfolio from scratch, a year-end review is a focused delta check: what changed this year, what was missed, and what needs action before January 1.

The distinction matters because most companies either run a full audit once, usually before a funding round, or never. The year-end review fills the gap between those events. A good Chief IP Officer puts it on the calendar every year as a fixed part of how they operate.

## why do most companies skip the year-end IP review?

Most companies skip the year-end IP review because nobody owns it. Finance has the CFO. Compliance has the General Counsel. IP sits in a gap between legal, engineering, and the C-suite, and in that gap deadlines expire, inventions go unprotected, and licensing revenue disappears. Hayat Amin calls this the ownership vacuum. It's why 93% of patent portfolios generate zero licensing revenue, nobody with P&L accountability is watching them.

The second reason is cost perception. Founders assume an IP review requires outside counsel at $800 per hour. It does not. A fractional Chief IP Officer runs this review as part of their operating cadence, no separate engagement, no surprise invoice. The review pays for itself the first time it catches a maintenance fee deadline or an unassigned contractor invention.

## what are the 9 moves in a year-end IP review?

The nine moves below are sequenced so each one feeds the next. A Chief IP Officer completes them in one half-day session, with follow-up actions assigned before the end of December.

### 1. Audit patent maintenance fee deadlines for the next 12 months

Pull every granted patent and check which maintenance or renewal fees are due between January and December of the following year. A single missed deadline can abandon a patent permanently, and reinstatement, where available, costs 5 to 10 times the original fee. Flag every deadline, assign an owner, and set calendar reminders 90 days in advance.

### 2. Capture every invention disclosed and not disclosed during the year

Engineering teams create patentable inventions constantly. Most never get reported. Review every product release, feature launch, and R&D project from the past 12 months and run each through an invention disclosure form. Hayat Amin's Patent Mining Method, a structured interview with engineering leads, typically surfaces 3 to 5 patentable inventions per product team per year that would otherwise go unprotected.

### 3. Update the IP asset register

Every patent, trademark, trade secret, copyright registration, and domain name should appear in a single register with current status, owner, jurisdiction, and expiry date. If your register doesn't exist, build one. If it exists but hasn't been updated since the last funding round, it's already wrong. The register is what acquirers, investors, and licensees will request first in any due diligence process.

### 4. Review competitor patent filings from the past 12 months

Your competitors filed patents this year. Some of those filings may cover technology you use, plan to use, or assumed was unprotectable. A competitive patent review, filtering new publications by assignee, classification, and keyword, identifies freedom-to-operate risks before they become infringement claims. This is a 2-hour exercise with the right tools, and it regularly changes product roadmap decisions.

### 5. Check licensing agreement compliance and renewal dates

If your company licenses IP in or out, verify that every agreement is in compliance: royalty reports filed on time, usage within scope, and no upcoming termination or renewal triggers. Licensing disputes almost always start with a compliance gap that nobody noticed until the counterparty's audit found it. One Beyond Elevation client discovered during a year-end review that a licensee had been underreporting royalties by 22% for three consecutive quarters, a $340,000 recovery triggered by a single spreadsheet check.

### 6. Assess trade secret protections

Trade secrets are only protected if you can prove you took reasonable steps to keep them secret. Review who has access to confidential information, whether NDA coverage is current for all employees and contractors, and whether any departing employees had access to trade secrets without proper exit procedures. U.S. Defend Trade Secrets Act cases in 2026 show that courts increasingly require documented, active protection measures. A signature on an NDA on day one isn't enough on its own.

### 7. Verify contractor and employee IP assignments

Every person who wrote code, designed a product, or contributed to an invention this year should have a signed IP assignment agreement on file. Gaps in assignment are the number-one issue that kills deals during due diligence. Hayat Amin reminds founders that the cost of fixing an assignment gap after a contractor has left is 10 to 50 times the cost of getting the signature while they're still engaged.

### 8. Align the IP roadmap with next year's business strategy

If the company is planning to enter a new market, launch a new product, or raise capital in the coming year, the IP strategy must match. A year-end review is the moment to map planned filings, identify white-space opportunities, and budget for prosecution costs. Filing a patent 6 months after a product launch, instead of 6 months before, can mean the difference between a granted patent and a prior-art rejection based on your own public disclosure.

### 9. Update the IP valuation

If your company last valued its IP during the Series A, that number is wrong now. New patents, new licensing revenue, competitor moves, and market changes all affect what the portfolio is worth. An updated valuation gives the CFO accurate numbers for the balance sheet, the board a clearer picture of enterprise value, and the founder a stronger hand in any upcoming negotiation. Hayat Amin's rule is that an IP portfolio that hasn't been revalued in 12 months is a guess.

## FAQ

### How long does a year-end IP review take?

A fractional Chief IP Officer completes the core review in a half-day session, typically 4 to 6 hours. Follow-up actions like filing provisional applications or updating the IP register may take an additional 2 to 3 weeks depending on portfolio size and the number of gaps found.

### Is a year-end IP review the same as an IP audit?

No. A full IP audit maps an entire portfolio from scratch and typically costs $25,000 to $75,000. A year-end IP review is a focused delta check on what changed during the calendar year. It's faster, cheaper, and designed to be repeated annually as part of an operating cadence.

### Can a patent attorney run the year-end IP review?

A patent attorney can handle the technical side, maintenance fees, filing status, prosecution updates. The strategic side, competitor analysis, business alignment, valuation updates, licensing compliance, needs someone with operational and commercial context. That's why the review belongs to a Chief IP Officer, not a law firm.

### What happens if I miss a patent maintenance deadline?

In most jurisdictions, missing a maintenance fee deadline results in patent abandonment. Some offices allow reinstatement within 6 to 12 months at a penalty fee of 2 to 10 times the original amount. In other jurisdictions, the patent is permanently lost. The year-end review eliminates this risk by flagging every deadline 12 months in advance.

### Do I need a year-end IP review if my company has no patents?

Yes. IP includes trade secrets, trademarks, copyrights, proprietary data, and know-how. Even a company with zero patents likely has unprotected trade secrets, unsigned contractor agreements, or brand assets that need renewal. The year-end review covers the full IP spectrum: trademarks, trade secrets, copyrights and patents.
