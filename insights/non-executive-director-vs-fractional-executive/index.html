Non-Executive Director or Fractional Executive? One Advises. The Other Ships.

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-10-31

Most SMEs hire a non-executive director when they need a fractional executive. Here is the decision framework, the real costs, and when you need both.

A non-executive director advises. A fractional executive runs the function. That single distinction costs companies years of stalled execution when they pick the wrong one. According to the UK Institute of Directors (2025), 58 percent of SMEs with revenue between two and twenty million pounds have at least one NED on the board, yet fewer than 15 percent have a fractional executive in an operating role. Hayat Amin argues that this ratio is backwards: most companies at that stage need someone who ships, not someone who sits in a monthly board meeting and asks better questions.

The non-executive director vs fractional executive decision is the most consequential leadership hire most founders get wrong. Both cost a fraction of what a permanent executive costs. Both bring senior experience. But they solve different problems, and confusing the two leaves a critical function without an owner.

What Is a Non-Executive Director?

A non-executive director is a board member who works a few days a month, providing independent governance, strategic oversight, and accountability without managing day-to-day operations. NEDs attend board meetings, challenge the executive team's assumptions, and bring an outside perspective shaped by their own operating or investment experience. They do not hire staff, manage budgets, or own deliverables.

The NED role exists to protect shareholders and improve board-level decision-making. NEDs are mandatory in regulated industries such as financial services, healthcare, and energy. For growth-stage companies, a good NED brings a network, credibility with investors, and the hard questions nobody internally wants to ask.

A NED does not run your finance function, restructure your IP portfolio, or deploy AI agents across your back office. A non-executive director advises on whether those things should happen. They do not make them happen.

What does a fractional executive do that a non-executive director cannot?

A fractional executive embeds in your company two to three days a week and runs a specific function, finance, intellectual property, AI operations, or general management, as if they'd been hired permanently, but at a fraction of the cost. They own outcomes, manage teams, build processes, and answer for the numbers their function produces. Hayat Amin calls a fractional executive an operator. They show up, do the work, and leave the function stronger than they found it.

A NED sits above the executive team. A fractional executive sits inside it. A fractional CFO closes the books, builds the forecast, and presents to the board. A fractional Chief IP Officer runs the patent filing roadmap, negotiates licensing deals, and values the portfolio for fundraising. An AI Operations operator deploys agents, measures ROI, and eliminates manual processes across the back office.

Accountability for execution is what separates the two roles.

How do you decide between a non-executive director and a fractional executive?

The decision comes down to one question: does your company need governance or execution? If you need someone to hold the CEO accountable, bring investor credibility, and challenge strategy at board level, hire a NED. If you have a function with no owner, no process, and no one doing the work, hire a fractional executive. Hayat Amin developed the Governance-vs-Execution Test at Beyond Elevation to help founders make this call in under five minutes.

Hire a non-executive director when:

  • Your board lacks independent oversight or specific sector expertise
  • Investors or regulators require an independent director
  • You need someone to challenge the executive team's blind spots
  • The function already has a competent owner and just needs strategic direction at board level

Hire a fractional executive when:

  • A critical function, finance, IP, AI operations, has no senior owner
  • The founder is personally doing CFO or CTO work alongside running the company
  • You need someone to build the process, rather than advise on whether to build one
  • You can't justify a salary of £150,000 or more for a role on the payroll full time, when the function only needs two to three days a week

What does each role cost in 2026?

A non-executive director in the UK typically costs between fifteen and forty thousand pounds a year, depending on company size, sector, and the NED's profile. Most NEDs attend ten to twelve board meetings a year, plus preparation, committee work, and ad hoc calls. FTSE 250 NEDs earn more, but for SMEs the range holds steady.

A fractional executive typically costs between three and eight thousand pounds a month, roughly thirty-six to ninety-six thousand pounds a year, for two to three days a week of embedded operational work. The cost is higher than a NED in absolute terms, but the output is different. Hayat Amin reminds founders that a fractional CFO who closes the books in two days instead of twelve is a margin improvement that pays for itself by month two.

The real comparison is fractional versus full-time. A full-time CFO costs £120,000 to £200,000 in salary alone, plus benefits, equity, and recruitment fees. A fractional CFO delivers the same critical outputs at a fraction of that cost, and steps aside once the function no longer needs that level of seniority.

When does a company need both?

Some companies need both a non-executive director and a fractional executive at the same time. A Series A company with investor board seats but no CFO needs a fractional CFO to build the finance function and a NED to provide independent governance. A company preparing for exit needs a fractional Chief IP Officer to value and structure the patent portfolio and a NED with M&A experience to guide the board through the sale process.

The mistake is hiring one when you need the other. A NED cannot close the month-end books. A fractional CFO cannot provide the independent board-level challenge investors expect. The Bottom Line

The choice between a non-executive director and a fractional executive depends on whether your company needs someone to ask better questions or someone to do the work, not on seniority, cost, or prestige. Most companies between two and twenty million pounds in revenue need execution first, a function built, a process owned, a senior operator accountable for the numbers. That's a fractional executive. The NED comes later, or alongside, once the function has an owner and the board needs independent oversight.

FAQ

Can a non-executive director also be a fractional executive?

Technically yes, but it defeats the purpose. A NED's value comes from independence. They challenge the executive team from outside. If they also run a function, they lose that independence. Most governance codes and investor expectations keep the roles separate. If you need both governance and execution, hire two people.

Is a fractional executive cheaper than a non-executive director?

No. A fractional executive typically costs more in absolute terms, thirty-six to ninety-six thousand pounds a year versus fifteen to forty thousand for a NED. But the outputs are different, so the comparison is misleading. A NED provides governance. A fractional executive provides execution. Compare the fractional cost against the salary of the permanent hire it replaces, not against a NED fee.

When should a startup hire its first non-executive director?

Most startups benefit from a NED after raising institutional funding, when the board formalises and investors expect independent oversight. Before that point, a fractional executive, particularly a fractional CFO, usually delivers more immediate value by building the finance function that makes the company investable.
