Startup Treasury Management Is Not a Series C Problem. It Is Week-One CFO Work.

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-11-21

Most startups keep all their cash in one bank account with no sweep, no policy, and no one watching. Startup treasury management is a fractional CFO's first build. Here is what it looks like.

Your startup raised $2M and deposited it in one bank account. No sweep. No counterparty diversification. No yield. No written policy. That is not startup treasury management. It is a cheque waiting to bounce the month a wire gets stuck.

Hayat Amin, who has built startup treasury management systems across three prior exits, argues the problem is simpler than founders make it: nobody on the team owns cash. According to the Federal Reserve's 2023 Small Business Credit Survey, 48% of firms with under $5M in revenue have no formal cash management procedures. For venture-backed startups, the number is worse. Most operate with zero treasury controls until a CFO shows up and builds them from scratch.

What Is Startup Treasury Management?

Startup treasury management is the system that controls where your cash sits, what it earns, how fast you can access it, and what happens if one counterparty fails. It is not a spreadsheet. It is a policy, a set of accounts, and a weekly rhythm a fractional CFO runs alongside the rest of the finance function.

When Silicon Valley Bank collapsed in March 2023, an estimated 2,500 startups discovered their entire operating cash was concentrated in a single counterparty with 93% of deposits uninsured. The startups that survived had one thing in common: a treasury structure that distributed cash across multiple banks and instruments before the crisis hit.

At Beyond Elevation, the first thing a fractional CFO builds is exactly this structure. Not because it is glamorous. Because it is the highest-consequence, lowest-effort financial control a startup can have.

Why Do Most Startups Have No Treasury Policy?

Most startups have no treasury policy because nobody on the founding team has a finance background, and the bookkeeper who handles the books was never asked to manage cash. Treasury is a CFO responsibility, and without a CFO, it simply does not get done. The money goes into the account the bank opened at incorporation and stays there.

Hayat Amin says the pattern repeats in almost every engagement: founders move fast on product and slow on cash. By the time a CFO walks in, the company has $3M sitting in a zero-interest checking account with one bank, one signer, and no backup plan.

This is not carelessness. It is a gap in the org chart. Treasury is CFO work, and most startups do not have a CFO until the board forces the hire.

What Does a Fractional CFO Build for Startup Treasury Management in Week One?

A fractional CFO builds a four-bucket cash structure in the first five working days. The structure separates operating cash from reserve cash, puts every idle dollar into yield-bearing instruments, and ensures no single bank holds a dangerous share of total cash.

Hayat Amin's Cash Deployment Framework breaks startup treasury management into four buckets:

Bucket 1: Operating Account

This holds 60 to 90 days of burn. It pays payroll, vendors, and recurring expenses. It stays liquid and accessible same-day. The balance is sized to runway, not habit. Most startups keep far too much in this account because nobody calculated the right number.

Bucket 2: Reserve Account

This holds three to six months of burn in a high-yield savings or money market account. It earns yield while remaining accessible within one to two business days. It acts as the buffer if a revenue shock or customer churn hits the operating account hard.

Bucket 3: Yield Account

Cash above the reserve threshold goes into short-duration instruments with 30 to 90 day maturities. In the current rate environment, this bucket earns 4% to 5% annualised. On $2M of idle cash, that is $80K to $100K a year the company was leaving on the table.

Bucket 4: Counterparty Diversification

No single bank holds more than 40% to 50% of total cash. The FDIC insures $250K per depositor per institution. If your startup has $3M in one account, $2.75M of it is uninsured. A second bank relationship, opened in week one, eliminates the single-counterparty risk that wiped out companies during the SVB collapse.

How Much Cash Should Be in Each Bucket?

The allocation depends on burn rate, revenue predictability, and fundraising timeline, but the starting framework for a post-seed startup with $2M to $5M in cash splits roughly 25-30% operating, 30-35% reserve, and 25-35% yield, with no single bank holding more than half of total cash.

Operating account: 25% to 30% of total cash. This covers 60 to 90 days of expenses with no revenue assumption.

Reserve account: 30% to 35%. Enough to absorb a bad quarter without touching the yield bucket.

Yield bucket: 25% to 35%. Every dollar here earns a return. The maturities are staggered so something comes due every 30 days.

Counterparty split: At minimum, two banking relationships. Three is better if total cash exceeds $5M. The operating account and reserve account should be at different institutions.

The ratios shift as the company scales. A pre-revenue startup with an 18-month runway allocates more to operating and reserve. A company with $1M in monthly recurring revenue can push more into yield. A fractional CFO recalibrates these buckets every quarter as part of the cash flow review.

What Does a Startup Treasury Policy Actually Cover?

A startup treasury policy is a one-page to three-page document that answers five questions: who can move cash, where cash can be held, what instruments are permitted, what the concentration limits are, and who reviews the policy quarterly. That is the entire document. It does not need to be complicated. It needs to be written, signed by a founder or board member, and followed.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that the policy is not paperwork. It is what stops a junior employee from moving $500K to a new account without authorisation. It is what proves to investors during due diligence that the company manages cash like a grown-up business.

Investors check for this. A board that asks where the cash sits and what the counterparty exposure is expects a one-page answer. If nobody can produce it, the CFO slot just became urgent.

When Does a Startup Actually Need a Treasury Function?

A startup needs a treasury function immediately after any funding event that puts more than $500K in the company's account. That is seed stage for most startups. Not Series C. Not when the board asks. Not after a bank fails. The trigger events that make it urgent are straightforward.

You raised a round. Any round above $500K means you are holding other people's money. Manage it.

Your cash exceeds FDIC limits. More than $250K in one account means uninsured exposure. This is fixable in a day.

You have no yield on idle cash. If your checking account earns 0% and short-duration instruments pay 4% to 5%, you are donating money to your bank.

You are preparing for due diligence. Every acquirer and growth investor asks about cash management. Having a policy is the minimum. Having a structure is what earns the meeting.

FAQ

How much does startup treasury management cost to set up?

Opening additional bank accounts is free. A money market account has no setup fee. The only cost is the CFO time to design the structure, write the policy, and execute the transfers. That is roughly 10 to 15 hours of work. A fractional CFO doing this as part of a broader engagement completes it in the first week at no incremental cost beyond the retainer.

Can a startup founder manage treasury without a CFO?

Technically, yes. A founder can open two bank accounts, move excess cash into a money market fund, and write a one-page policy. The problem is that nobody does it without someone whose job it is. Treasury falls off the priority list behind product, hiring, and sales every single week until the gap becomes a crisis.

What return should a startup expect from a treasury structure?

At current rates, a startup with $2M in idle cash moved from a zero-interest checking account into a staggered money market and short-duration structure earns $80K to $100K per year. That is a junior hire funded entirely by cash that was previously earning nothing.

Does treasury management matter for pre-revenue startups?

It matters more for pre-revenue startups. When the company has no revenue, cash is the only asset. Every month that cash sits unmanaged, the company loses yield and carries uninsured counterparty risk. The less revenue a startup has, the more its startup treasury management structure matters.
