Trademark Strategy for Startups: The $275 Filing That Outlasts Every Patent in Your Portfolio

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-10-22

A trademark costs 1% of what a patent costs to file, lasts indefinitely, and survives a complete product pivot. Yet most seed-stage tech companies file patents before a single trademark. Here is the trademark strategy playbook that fixes the most expensive sequencing mistake in startup IP.

A trademark lasts forever. A patent lasts 20 years. Yet 73% of seed-stage tech companies file at least one patent before they file a single trademark. That is the most expensive sequencing mistake in startup IP — and the cheapest one to fix.

According to the International Trademark Association (INTA), companies that register trademarks in their first year of operation retain 2.5x more brand equity through a pivot or acquisition than those that delay. Hayat Amin argues that trademark strategy is the single most neglected discipline inside tech company IP portfolios. "I have audited over 200 startup IP portfolios," Hayat Amin says. "Fewer than 15% had a trademark strategy. Every single one had a patent budget."

That gap is not an oversight. It is a structural vulnerability that compounds every year you delay.

What Is a Trademark Strategy and Why Do Tech Startups Need One?

A trademark strategy is a systematic plan for identifying, registering, monitoring, and enforcing the brand assets — names, logos, product marks, and slogans — that customers associate with your company. For tech startups, it is the only IP protection that survives a complete product pivot.

Patents protect inventions. Copyrights protect code. Trademarks protect the name customers type into Google when they want your product. If a competitor registers your brand name in a new market before you do, you lose the right to use your own name in that territory. It happens every year to companies that should have known better.

The cost to register a trademark with the USPTO is $250–$350 per class. The cost to litigate a trademark dispute averages $375,000 for cases that go to trial, according to the AIPLA's 2025 Economic Survey. That is a 1,000:1 ratio between prevention and cure.

When Should a Startup File Its First Trademark?

Before your first customer sees your brand name. The moment you use a name in commerce — on a website, an app store listing, or a pitch deck shared externally — you create common-law trademark rights. But common-law rights are geographically limited and nearly impossible to enforce at scale. Federal registration changes the equation entirely.

Hayat Amin's rule is direct: "File the trademark before the pitch deck. Not because you are ready to enforce it, but because a VC's associate will Google your company name during due diligence. If someone else owns the mark, that is a line item in the risk section of the investment memo." That is not hypothetical. Beyond Elevation has seen three Series A deals delayed — not killed, but delayed by weeks — because the company name was registered by a third party in the EU.

The filing timeline for US startups:

Pre-launch (month -3): File an intent-to-use (ITU) application for your company name in your core class. Cost: $250.

Launch (month 0): Convert the ITU to a use-based registration once you have evidence of commerce.

Month 6–12: File product-name marks for your flagship product. If you have a named API or developer platform, file that too.

International expansion: File in target markets 6 months before you sell there. First-to-file jurisdictions — China, most of the EU — do not care who used the name first. The first party to register owns the mark.

What Does a Complete Trademark Strategy Cover?

A complete trademark strategy for a tech company covers four layers: corporate brand, product names, feature and platform marks, and defensive registrations. Most startups stop at layer one. Hayat Amin's Four-Layer Brand Protection Framework is the diagnostic Beyond Elevation runs on every client portfolio that includes consumer-facing products.

Layer 1 — Corporate brand. The company name and logo. This is the minimum. Every startup should file this in its home jurisdiction and its top two revenue markets.

Layer 2 — Product names. If your product has a name distinct from the company, it needs its own mark. Slack, Copilot, and Gemini are all separately registered marks. If your product name becomes generic — "just Google it" — you lose the mark entirely.

Layer 3 — Feature and platform marks. API names, developer ecosystem brands, named features that users associate with your product. These are the marks that create switching costs. When developers build on "your-name API," that name is a protectable asset.

Layer 4 — Defensive registrations. Common misspellings, adjacent classes, and first-to-file jurisdictions where cybersquatting is common. Costs $1,500–$3,000 per year for a typical startup. Saves $50,000–$200,000 per dispute avoided.

How Much Does a Trademark Portfolio Cost to Maintain?

A startup-stage trademark portfolio — three to five marks across two jurisdictions — costs between $2,000 and $5,000 per year in filing and maintenance fees. That is less than one month of a single engineer's compensation. At Series A, a well-managed trademark portfolio adds between $4,000 and $12,000 per year as product marks and international filings expand.

Compare that to patent prosecution. A single utility patent costs $15,000–$30,000 to file and prosecute. Annual maintenance fees add $5,000–$10,000 over the patent's life. Most startups file 3–5 patents before they file a single trademark, spending $60,000–$150,000 on patent prosecution while leaving their brand name — the asset customers actually recognise — unprotected.

Hayat Amin says the math is not complicated: "A trademark costs 2% of what a patent costs to file. It lasts indefinitely instead of 20 years. And it is the only IP asset that survives a complete product pivot. If you can only afford one IP filing this quarter, file the trademark."

What Happens When You Skip Trademark Protection?

Three scenarios play out repeatedly in startup portfolios that neglect trademarks.

Scenario 1 — Name squatting. A competitor or domain squatter registers your brand name in a market you have not yet entered. Recovering the mark costs $50,000–$200,000 in legal fees and 18–24 months. In first-to-file jurisdictions like China, you may never recover it.

Scenario 2 — Brand confusion. A larger company launches a product with the same or confusingly similar name. Without a registered mark, your options are limited to expensive litigation with uncertain outcomes. With a registered mark, you send a cease-and-desist letter and the matter resolves in weeks.

Scenario 3 — Exit discount. You reach an acquisition and the buyer's due diligence team flags unregistered marks as a risk. The IP section of the purchase agreement requires representations about trademark ownership. Unregistered marks create carve-outs, indemnities, or price adjustments. Hayat Amin proved this during one portfolio review where a missing trademark registration in the EU reduced the acquirer's initial offer by $800,000 — the estimated cost to clear the mark post-close, plus a risk premium.

How Does a Chief IP Officer Manage Trademark Strategy?

A Chief IP Officer owns the entire trademark lifecycle: clearance searches before naming decisions, filing strategy across jurisdictions, renewal management, monitoring for infringement, and enforcement. A fractional Chief IP Officer integrates trademark strategy into the broader IP portfolio alongside patents, trade secrets, and data assets — so nothing falls through the gaps between separate outside counsel.

The practical difference is timing. A patent attorney files trademarks reactively — after a problem surfaces. A Chief IP Officer files proactively — before the brand enters a new market, before a product launches, before a competitor files first. The proactive approach costs 10% of the reactive one.

Most companies do not need a full-time Chief IP Officer for trademark management alone. But when trademark strategy sits inside a broader IP portfolio that includes patents, licensing, and data protection, the fractional CIPO model — one to two days per week — covers all four layers without the $300,000–$500,000 annual cost of a full-time hire. FAQ

How long does it take to register a trademark?

In the US, expect 8–12 months from filing to registration if there are no oppositions. An intent-to-use application provides priority from the filing date, so you are protected while the application is pending. In the EU, the process takes 4–6 months through the EUIPO.

Can you trademark a software feature name?

Yes, if the name identifies the source of a product or service and is not merely descriptive. Feature names that become associated with a specific company — like Copilot or Siri — are registrable. Generic or purely descriptive terms are not.

What is the difference between a trademark and a patent for a tech company?

A patent protects an invention — how something works — for 20 years. A trademark protects a brand identifier — a name, logo, or slogan — indefinitely. Patents block competitors from copying your technology. Trademarks block competitors from copying your brand. Most tech companies need both, but the trademark should come first because it is cheaper, faster, and lasts longer.

Do you need a lawyer to file a trademark?

You can file directly with the USPTO for $250–$350 per class. However, a clearance search before filing — to confirm no one else owns a similar mark — costs $500–$1,500 and prevents the most common rejection reason. A fractional Chief IP Officer manages this alongside your broader IP strategy at a fraction of outside counsel rates.

How many trademarks does a typical tech startup need?

At launch, one to two marks — company name and primary product name — in your home jurisdiction. By Series A, three to five marks across two to three jurisdictions. The number grows with your product line and international footprint, but the annual cost rarely exceeds $12,000 until you reach enterprise scale.
