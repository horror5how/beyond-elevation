When to Replace a Fractional Executive With a Full-Time Hire: The 4-Signal Test

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-10-01

Most companies replace their fractional executive too early. Here is the 4-signal test that tells you when the role is ready for a full-time hire and when switching destroys value.

Replace your fractional executive with a full-time hire when the role demands more than three days per week of dedicated attention, the function has moved from build to run, and total compensation stays under 2.5 times the fractional retainer. Until all three conditions converge, switching destroys more value than it creates.

According to Korn Ferry's 2025 CEO and C-Suite Succession report, 40% of externally hired C-suite executives fail within 18 months. Hayat Amin argues the failure rate climbs higher when the hire replaces a fractional operator who was still mid-build. "A fractional executive who is building your finance function, your IP strategy, or your AI operations is not a placeholder," Hayat Amin says. "They are the architect. You do not fire the architect halfway through construction and replace them with a building manager."

The question is not whether to go full-time eventually. Most functions do. The question is when. Six months too early costs 12 months of progress.

Why Do Most Companies Replace Their Fractional Executive Too Early?

Most companies make the fractional-to-full-time switch too early because boards equate headcount with commitment and mistake active function-building for steady-state operations. The result is a full-time salary paying someone to manage a function that has not finished being built, owned by a person who has never constructed it.

Three forces push the switch before the function is ready. First, investor pressure: some VCs still treat fractional executives as a red flag, reading "part-time" as "not serious." According to a 2025 Sapphire Ventures LP survey, 31% of Series A investors listed a full-time CFO as a board-readiness criterion regardless of whether the function needed one. Second, founder ego: a full-time C-suite hire feels like a milestone. Third, availability bias: when a strong candidate appears on the market, the temptation to lock them in overrides timing discipline.

Hayat Amin's counter is direct: "The board that forces a full-time hire before the function is built gets a caretaker when they needed a builder. Caretakers maintain systems. Builders create them. You cannot maintain what does not exist yet."

What Are the 4 Signals That a Role Is Ready for a Full-Time Executive?

A fractional executive role is ready for a full-time replacement when four conditions converge: the workload consistently exceeds three days per week, the function's core systems are built and documented, the role has shifted from strategic design to operational management, and the total cost of a full-time hire is less than 2.5 times the current fractional retainer. Miss any one, and the transition is premature.

Signal 1: Workload exceeds three days per week for three consecutive months. A fractional executive working one to two days per week is operating at the right altitude: strategic decisions, key meetings, framework design. When the workload consistently exceeds three days, the function has grown past what fractional delivery can serve efficiently. Track this honestly. Calendar time is not the same as decision load.

Signal 2: Core systems are built, documented, and running without the fractional's direct intervention. Hayat Amin calls this the "holiday test" and uses it as the second gate in what Beyond Elevation internally calls the Role Maturity Framework. If the fractional executive takes two weeks off and the function keeps operating, the build phase is complete. If it stalls, you are still in architect mode and a full-time manager will inherit an unfinished house.

Signal 3: The role has shifted from design to execution. Fractional executives are overqualified and overpriced for steady-state operations. A fractional CFO who designed your reporting stack, built your board pack, and structured your data room is not the person you need processing month-end close every month. When 70% or more of the work is repeatable execution, the role is ready for a full-time operator at a lower seniority and lower cost.

Signal 4: Total compensation for the full-time hire stays under 2.5 times the fractional retainer. A fractional CFO at 4,000 pounds per month costs 48,000 pounds per year. The full-time replacement including salary, benefits, equity, bonus, and recruitment fee will cost 120,000 to 180,000 pounds in total. If the function genuinely needs full-time attention and all three signals above are green, that multiple makes sense. If you are paying the premium just to have someone in a seat five days a week, you are buying presence, not performance.

What Does the Full Cost Comparison Actually Look Like?

The real cost of replacing a fractional executive with a full-time hire runs 3 to 5 times the visible salary once you add recruitment, onboarding, ramp time, and the probability of a failed hire. Most boards compare the fractional retainer to the base salary and stop there. That comparison is incomplete and misleading.

A typical fractional CFO retainer in 2026 runs 3,000 to 8,000 pounds per month for two to three days per week. The full-time replacement costs 90,000 to 150,000 pounds in base salary plus 20 to 30% in benefits, equity, and bonus. Add a 30,000 to 50,000 pound recruitment fee, three to six months of reduced productivity during onboarding, and the 40% failure rate Korn Ferry documents. The expected cost of a single full-time hire is 180,000 to 320,000 pounds in the first year.

Hayat Amin reminds founders that this math changes in one specific scenario: "When the function needs daily decisions that cannot wait for the fractional's next day on-site, the cost of delay exceeds the cost of the hire. That is the breakeven signal, and it is the only one that justifies paying the premium before all four signals are green."

When Should You Keep the Fractional Executive Permanently?

Some functions never need a full-time executive, and that is not a compromise but a strategy. For many companies between 5 million and 50 million pounds in revenue, the correct long-term answer is a permanent fractional retainer. According to the Fractional Executive Association's 2026 benchmark data, 34% of fractional engagements that started as interim became permanent retainers lasting more than two years.

Three conditions make permanent fractional the right structure. The function needs senior strategic input but generates less than three days of operational work per week. The company operates in a sector where the expertise required, whether IP strategy, AI operations, or specialist finance, is scarce and expensive at the full-time level. Or the company is pre-exit and needs operator-grade talent without adding headcount to the cap table.

Hayat Amin's rule is blunt: "If the function is strategic and the workload is episodic, permanent fractional beats permanent full-time every time. You get the same operator at 40% of the cost. The only thing you lose is the illusion of control that comes from seeing someone at a desk five days a week."

How Should You Structure the Handover?

The transition from fractional to full-time should be a planned handover spanning 90 days, not a replacement event. The best transitions follow a specific three-phase sequence that protects institutional knowledge, transfers relationships, and ensures function continuity through the switch.

Month 1: Documentation. The fractional executive writes the role specification, defines KPIs, and documents every system, process, and relationship the function depends on. This document becomes the onboarding blueprint for the full-time hire.

Month 2: Overlap. The full-time hire starts alongside the fractional. Two people, one function, 30 days of overlap. The fractional introduces the new hire to every internal stakeholder and external partner. They walk through live decisions together so the new hire understands not just the systems but the judgment calls behind them.

Month 3: Step-back. The fractional drops to one day per week, available for questions and course correction. By the end of month three, the full-time hire owns the function completely.

FAQ

How long should a fractional executive engagement last before considering a full-time hire?

There is no fixed timeline. Most functions take 6 to 18 months to move from build to run. The 4-signal test matters more than calendar time. Some functions hit all four signals in 9 months. Others never do, and permanent fractional is the correct answer.

Can a fractional executive become the full-time hire?

Yes, and it works roughly 60% of the time. The risk is that the operator who built the function may not want to manage it daily. Have the conversation early. If the fractional wants the full-time role and the signals are green, you skip the recruitment risk entirely.

What happens to the function during the transition gap?

Nothing, if you plan correctly. The 90-day overlap model eliminates the gap entirely. The mistake is treating it as a clean swap, ending the fractional on Friday and starting the full-time hire on Monday. That approach destroys continuity and loses institutional knowledge that took months to build.

Do investors still prefer full-time executives over fractional ones?

Less than they used to. According to the 2026 State of Fractional Work report by A.Team, 72% of Series A and B investors now accept fractional C-suite arrangements when the function delivers measurable output. Results override org charts.
