Working Capital Management for Startups: Revenue Grows, Cash Disappears, and Nobody Tells the Founder Why

Hayat Amin · Updated 2026-10-12

Working capital management is the reason profitable startups go broke. Here are the three levers a fractional CFO pulls in week one to stop growth from eating your cash.

Working capital management is the discipline of ensuring your startup has enough cash flowing in to cover the cash flowing out, on time, every week, without a panic call to your investors. According to a 2025 U.S. Bank study, 82% of small business failures involve cash flow problems, and the majority of those businesses were generating revenue when they died. Hayat Amin argues that working capital management is the single most dangerous blind spot in startup finance: "Founders track MRR, CAC, and LTV religiously. Almost none of them can tell you their cash conversion cycle. That is the number that kills companies."

This is a structural trap, not a liquidity crisis, and it catches good businesses growing fast as easily as bad ones. The faster you grow, the more cash your working capital absorbs, and if nobody manages the timing gap between when you pay and when you collect, growth itself becomes the thing that bankrupts you.

What is working capital management for startups?

Working capital management for startups is the process of monitoring and optimising the gap between current assets (cash, receivables, prepaid expenses) and current liabilities (payables, accrued costs, short-term debt) so the business never runs out of cash despite being profitable on paper. It is the CFO function that keeps the company solvent between the moment you spend money and the moment your customers pay you.

For startups, working capital management matters more than for established businesses because the margin of error is zero. A company with 18 months of runway and 60-day payment terms from enterprise customers can burn through 4 months of that runway just waiting to collect revenue it has already booked. Multiply that across growth, more deals, more receivables, more cash trapped in the timing gap, and revenue climbs while the bank balance drops.

Why does working capital kill startups that are growing?

Working capital problems accelerate in direct proportion to revenue growth because the mechanism is structural, not operational. You pay your team, your infrastructure, and your suppliers now. Your customers pay you 30, 60, or 90 days later. Every new customer you sign increases the amount of cash locked in that gap. This is the working capital trap, and it punishes the fastest-growing companies hardest.

A concrete example. A SaaS company signs a £200K annual contract. The customer pays quarterly in arrears, 30 days after invoice. That means the company spends 4 months of operational cost before the first payment arrives. At a 70% gross margin, the company needs roughly £60K of free cash to service that single contract before it collects anything. Sign 5 contracts in a quarter and the business needs £300K of free cash just to fund growth it already closed.

Hayat Amin's rule on this is blunt: "If you cannot fund 90 days of growth from operating cash flow, you do not have a cash problem. You have a working capital management problem. Those are different diagnoses with different fixes." Beyond Elevation's fractional CFOs diagnose the difference in the first week of every engagement, because the fix for a revenue problem looks nothing like the fix for a timing problem.

What are the 3 working capital levers a fractional CFO pulls?

Working capital management comes down to three levers that control the timing gap between cash out and cash in. Every fractional CFO engagement at Beyond Elevation starts by measuring and optimising all three, a process Hayat Amin calls the Cash Conversion Sprint, inside the first 30 days.

Lever 1: Receivables, get paid faster. The single highest-impact move in working capital management is reducing the time between invoicing and collection. Average days sales outstanding (DSO) for startups selling to enterprise runs 45 to 65 days. The target is under 30. Tactics that work immediately: invoice on the day the service is delivered, not at month-end. Offer a 2% early-payment discount for settlement within 10 days. Move enterprise customers to annual prepayment with a 5 to 10% discount, one collections cycle replaced by a single upfront payment. Automate reminders at day 1, day 7, and day 14 after invoice. Hayat Amin showed one portfolio company that moving from net-60 to net-30 with a 2% early-payment incentive freed £1.2M of cash that had been trapped in receivables, without raising a single pound of new capital.

Lever 2: Payables, pay later, strategically. The second lever extends the time you take to pay your own suppliers. This does not mean paying late. It means negotiating payment terms that match your collection cycle. If your DSO is 45 days and your days payable outstanding (DPO) is 15 days, you are funding a 30-day gap from your own cash. Negotiating DPO to 30 or 45 days closes that gap immediately. Renegotiate supplier terms annually, most suppliers extend from net-15 to net-30 when asked. Consolidate vendor payments to a single monthly run instead of ad-hoc processing. Use corporate cards with 30-day billing cycles for smaller expenses, which is a free, built-in payables extension.

Lever 3: Revenue structure, fix the cash architecture. The third lever is structural: redesign how and when customers pay so that cash arrives before or alongside the cost of delivery. SaaS companies can switch from monthly billing to annual prepayment. Service businesses can charge upfront deposits of 30 to 50%. Marketplace businesses can hold settlement for 7 to 14 days. Each change restructures the business model so that working capital funds itself rather than draining the balance sheet. Beyond Elevation has rebuilt payment architectures across SaaS, professional services, and marketplace models. The pattern is the same: move cash upstream, and growth stops eating your runway.

How do you calculate your cash conversion cycle?

The cash conversion cycle (CCC) is the single number that measures working capital management effectiveness. It tells you how many days elapse between spending cash on operations and collecting cash from customers. A lower CCC means less cash trapped in the business and more available for growth, hiring, or extending your 13-week cash runway.

The formula: CCC = DSO + DIO − DPO. Days sales outstanding (DSO) measures how long customers take to pay you. Days inventory outstanding (DIO) measures how long it takes to convert work-in-progress into a deliverable. Days payable outstanding (DPO) measures how long you take to pay suppliers. A SaaS startup with 50 DSO, 0 DIO, and 20 DPO has a cash conversion cycle of 30 days, so every pound of revenue takes 30 days to become cash in the bank. For a business spending £100K per month, that 30-day gap locks £100K of cash permanently. Reduce the CCC by 15 days and you free £50K.

Hayat Amin's benchmark: "A CCC under 30 days means you can fund growth from operations. Over 45 and you are borrowing from future cash to fund today's work. Over 60 and you are on a clock." Beyond Elevation tracks CCC weekly for every fractional CFO engagement, because cash position changes faster than most founders realise.

The cost of working capital management

A fractional CFO engagement that includes working capital management typically costs £3,000 to £6,000 per month for two days per week. Compare that to the cost of the problem: a single enterprise customer paying 30 days late on a £200K contract costs more in trapped cash than three months of fractional CFO fees. Most working capital engagements pay for themselves within the first 60 days through freed cash alone, before any revenue impact.

The real cost comparison is the alternative, not the fee. Raising £500K in equity to cover a working capital gap that should not exist dilutes the founders permanently. A fractional CFO who reduces DSO by 20 days might free that same £500K without a single share changing hands. Hayat Amin reminds founders: "The cheapest capital in your business is the capital already earned and not yet collected. A fractional CFO who recovers it costs a fraction of the dilution you would take raising the same amount."

FAQ

What is working capital management in simple terms?

Working capital management is the process of making sure your business always has enough cash to operate by controlling the timing of when you collect from customers, when you pay suppliers, and how you structure payments. It is the gap between cash in and cash out, and managing it well means never running out of money despite being profitable.

Why is working capital management important for startups?

Startups have thin cash buffers and long payment cycles, which means the gap between spending and collecting cash is proportionally larger than for established businesses. Fast growth amplifies this gap because every new customer increases the cash trapped in receivables. Without active working capital management, growth itself becomes the cause of cash crises.

What is a good cash conversion cycle for a startup?

Below 30 days is healthy for most software and services startups. Between 30 and 45 days is manageable with sufficient runway. Above 45 days signals a working capital management problem that a fractional CFO should address before the next fundraise. Amazon runs a negative CCC, they collect before they pay. Most startups should aim for 15 to 25 days.

Can working capital management replace fundraising?

In some cases, yes. If a company is raising capital specifically to fund a cash timing gap rather than to invest in product or headcount, working capital management can eliminate the need entirely. Beyond Elevation has seen companies cancel bridge rounds after a 20-day DSO reduction freed the cash they would have raised at 15 to 20% dilution. The discipline is cheaper than the equity.
